\section{The Cosmological Context}
\label{sec:cosmological_context}

Neutral (atomic) hydrogen (HI) plays an important role in cosmology and structure formation processes. Its distribution follows the underlying matter density field, and for most of the cosmic history it constitutes the content of baryons to fuel star formation. This makes it a powerful and novel tracer of the large scale structure (LSS) of the Universe~\citep[e.g.][]{review,Ansari_2012,Santos:2015,villa14}. While Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) observations~\citep{WMAP,planck:2018,ACT} and galaxy redshift surveys~\citep[e.g.][]{BOSS:2016} have significantly constrained the parameters of the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, some key questions are still unresolved. In particular, the fundamental nature of dark matter and dark energy is still unknown, and persistent tensions between different cosmological measurements have yet to be fully understood, including the recent evidence for evolving dark energy~\citep[e.g.][]{riess:2019,Verde:2019,wong:2019,DESIde}. Mapping the large scale distribution of HI and tracking its evolution over cosmic time offer a complementary approach to traditional galaxy surveys, by probing large volumes at high redshifts, and thus potentially providing stress tests for many cosmological models in new regimes~\citep[e.g.][]{bull:2015,villa15,obuljen_high redshift_2018,berti_constraining_2022,Villaescusa-Navarro:2016}.

The 21 cm line, which results from the forbidden spin flip transition within the hyperfine structure of the ground state of neutral hydrogen (see~\citet{Furlanetto:2006}), is redshifted by the cosmological expansion and can be observed at radio wavelengths. Many experiments, including interferometric arrays like CHIME~\citep{Bandura:2014gwa,CHIMEdetection}, CHORD, and HIRAX~\citep{Newburgh:2016mwi}, and single dish instruments such as GBT~\citep{Masui2013,Wolz2022} and FAST~\citep{Hu:2019okh}, aim to detect this signal using intensity mapping (IM) techniques~\citep{Bharadwaj:2000,Battye:2004,McQuinn:2005,Chang:2007,Seo:2009fq,Battye:2013,Kovetz:2017}. Several of these efforts have already achieved detections through cross-correlation with optical galaxy surveys~\citep{Chang2010,Masui2013,Anderson2018,Wolz2022,Cunnington:2022,Paul2023}.

Radio cosmology is also a key science objective for the SKA Observatory (SKAO),\footnote{\url{https://www.skao.int/}} which will comprise two major arrays: SKA-Low in Australia and SKA-Mid in South Africa. In particular, SKA-Mid, when operated in single-dish mode~\citep[e.g.][]{Santos:2015,Bacon:2018}, will be capable of conducting 21 cm IM surveys across cosmologically relevant scales out to redshift $z \sim 3$. Currently under construction, the SKAO has a working precursor, MeerKAT, which is already contributing through its cosmological Intensity mapping survey, MeerKLASS~\citep{Santos:2017}. Early results from MeerKAT data have been encouraging~\citep{Wang:2021,irfan2022}, including a first cross-correlation detection with WiggleZ galaxy data~\citep{Cunnington:2022uzo}.

Alongside several technical efforts mainly focused on foreground modeling and mitigation, refining the forecast capabilities of 21 cm IM, both as a standalone probe and in combination with other cosmological observables, is crucial (e.g.~\citep{carucci_cross-correlation_2017,berti_21cm_2023,berti24}). These forecasts are essential not only for strengthening the scientific case for 21 cm IM radio cosmology, but also in order to optimize the design and strategy of upcoming surveys.

\subsection{The expanding Universe}

Cosmology describes the origin, evolution, and large scale structure (LSS) of the Universe as a whole. On the largest scales, the Universe is observed to be homogeneous and isotropic, an assumption known as the cosmological principle. Together with the assumption of a spatially flat geometry , strongly supported by measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB)~\citep{planck:2018} , this fixes the spacetime metric to the Friedmann,Robertson,Walker (FRW) form,
\begin{equation}
    \text{d}s^2 = -c^2\,\text{d}t^2 + a^2(t)\,\text{d}\bm{r}^2,
\end{equation}
where $a(t)$ is the scale factor describing the relative  ratio of sizes of the Universe as a function of cosmic time $t$, conventionally normalized to $a=1$ today. Inserting this metric into the Einstein field equations relates the expansion rate to the energy content of the Universe, giving the Friedmann equation,
\begin{equation}
    H(t) \equiv \frac{1}{a}\frac{\text{d}a}{\text{d}t} = H_0\sqrt{\Om\,a^{-3} + \Omega_\Lambda},
\end{equation}
where $H_0\equiv H(t=1)$ is the Hubble constant, $\Om$ is the matter density parameter (dark matter plus baryons), and $\Omega_\Lambda$ is the dark-energy density parameter, with $\Om+\Omega_\Lambda=1$ for a flat Universe (radiation is a negligible contribution at the late times relevant to this thesis, and is dropped here for clarity). At early times, when $\Om a^{-3}\gg\Omega_\Lambda$, the Universe is matter dominated and $a(t)\propto t^{2/3}$; at late times, as $\Omega_\Lambda$ comes to dominate, the expansion instead accelerates towards $a(t)\propto e^{H_0 t}$. Current measurements give $\Om=0.315\pm0.007$~\citep{planck:2018}, with late-time probes broadly consistent with this value, though not without some persistent tension.

\subsection{Growth of structure}

The Universe is not perfectly homogeneous: small primordial density perturbations, seeded during an early inflationary epoch, grow under gravitational instability into the large scale structure observed today. It is convenient to describe these perturbations through the fractional matter overdensity,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(\bm{r},t) \equiv \frac{\rho(\bm{r},t) - \langle\rho(\bm{r},t)\rangle}{\langle\rho(\bm{r},t)\rangle},
\end{equation}
where $\langle\dots\rangle$ denotes an ensemble average. At times well after recombination and on sub-horizon scales, $\delta$ obeys, in Fourier space,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(a^2(t)\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\delta(\bm{k},t)\right) = 4\pi G\,a^2(t)\,\rho(t)\,\delta(\bm{k},t),
\end{equation}
sourced by the Poisson equation relating the gravitational potential $\Phi$ to $\delta$. Because this equation is linear in $\delta$, its solution factorizes into a part depending only on time and a part depending only on scale,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(\bm{k},a) = \frac{2k^2}{5\Om H_0^2}\,\mathcal{R}(\bm{k})\,T(k)\,D_+(a),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}(\bm{k})$ encodes the primordial (inflationary) fluctuations, $T(k)$ is the transfer function describing how different scales evolve differently around the epoch of matter-radiation equality, and $D_+(a)$ is the linear growth factor. The growth factor satisfies $D_+(a)\propto a$ during matter domination, and is suppressed at late times as dark-energy domination takes over and accelerated expansion begins to suppress further collapse;

\subsection{The power spectrum and Gaussianity}
The statistical properties of the density field are most commonly characterized through the (linear) matter power spectrum $\Pk$, defined via the Fourier transform of two point correlation function,
\begin{equation}
    (2\pi)^3\,\Pk\,\delta_\text{D}^3(\bm{k}+\bm{k}') = \langle\delta(\bm{k})\delta(\bm{k}')\rangle,
\end{equation}
with $\delta_\text{D}$ the Dirac delta function. The factorized form of $\delta(\bm{k},a)$ above implies $P_\text{Lin}(k,a)\propto T^2(k)\,D_+^2(a)$. A key property of the \emph{linear} density field is that, to very high precision, it is Gaussian: this is a direct consequence of the (approximately Gaussian) initial conditions from inflation, preserved under linear gravitational evolution. For a Gaussian field, the power spectrum is a \emph{complete} statistical description, every higher order correlator $\langle\delta(\bm{k}_1)\cdots\delta(\bm{k}_n)\rangle$ either vanishes (for odd $n$) or reduces to a sum of products of two point functions (for even $n$). The overall amplitude of the (linear) power spectrum is conventionally summarized by the parameter $\sige$, the root-mean-square overdensity in spheres of radius $8\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}$,
\begin{equation}
    \sige^2(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty \text{d}k\; k^2\, P_\text{Lin}(k,a)\, W^2(kR), \qquad R = 8\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc},
\end{equation}
with $W$ a top-hat filter in Fourier space. Together, $\Om$ and $\sige$ are two of the most fundamental parameters describing the amount and clustering strength of matter in the Universe, and much of observational cosmology, including the probes discussed later in this Chapter, is aimed at constraining them.

\subsection{The nonlinear scale}

The Gaussian, linear picture above is only valid while density fluctuations remain small, $|\delta|\ll1$. This breaks down once fluctuations become of order unity, defining a nonlinear wavenumber $k_\text{NL}$ through the dimensionless power spectrum,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^2(k_\text{NL}) \equiv \frac{k_\text{NL}^3}{2\pi^2}\,P_\text{Lin}(k_\text{NL}) \sim 1,
\end{equation}
which for present-day $\Lambda$CDM parameters occurs at $k_\text{NL}\sim0.2\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$~\citep{Takahashi:2012}. Beyond this scale, gravitational collapse drives the density field away from Gaussianity, producing the cosmic web of halos, filaments, and voids observed today; this is precisely the regime that the field level machine learning methods developed in this thesis are designed to model.

\subsection{The Jeans scale}

It is worth being careful to distinguish $k_\text{NL}$, which marks where \emph{gravitational} clustering of matter becomes nonlinear, from a second, physically distinct scale relevant once baryons (ordinary gas) are considered. The Jeans scale, below which gas pressure rather than gravity controls whether a region collapses., since baryonic structure and compact object formation is always a tug of war between the kinetic/thermal pressure produced by gas that is undergoing compactification due to gravitational collapse.

A simple, order of magnitude way to see where this scale comes from is to compare two timescales for a gas cloud of size $\lambda$ and density $\rho$: the time for it to collapse under its own gravity, and the time for a pressure (sound) wave to cross it and counteract that collapse. The gravitational free fall time scales as
\begin{equation}
    t_\text{gravity} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho}},
\end{equation}
while the sound-crossing time is
\begin{equation}
    t_\text{sound} \sim \frac{\lambda}{c_s},
\end{equation}
where $c_s$ is the sound speed of the gas. Collapse can proceed only if gravity acts faster than pressure can respond, i.e.\ $t_\text{gravity} \lesssim t_\text{sound}$. Setting the two timescales equal defines the Jeans length,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_J \sim \frac{c_s}{\sqrt{G\rho}},
\end{equation}
or, as a wavenumber, $k_J\sim 2\pi/\lambda_J$. (A more careful derivation, linearizing the fluid equations with pressure included, gives the same scaling with an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ prefactor~\citep{peacock_cosmological_1998}.) Below this scale, thermal pressure support resists collapse, and above it, self gravity wins and the gas can collapse along with the dark matter. Below this scale, matter structure gets washed out.

The Jeans scale and the nonlinear scale $k_\text{NL}$ therefore answer different questions. One is about gravitational clustering of matter in general (relevant for dark matter, which has no pressure support), the other about whether \emph{gas} specifically can collapse into that clustered structure , and both are relevant once modeling baryonic tracers such as neutral hydrogen. This is one of the reasons, elaborated in Section~\ref{sec:cosmo_sims}, that hydrodynamical simulations (rather than dark-matter-only $N$-body simulations) are needed to model such tracers realistically.

\subsection{Beyond linear theory: a brief note on perturbation theory}

Extending the description of structure formation beyond the linear regime is, in principle, possible order by order in what is known as cosmological perturbation theory, which introduces higher order corrections to the power spectrum (e.g.\ at ``1-loop'' order) and to new statistics such as the bispectrum,
\begin{equation}
    (2\pi)^3\,B(k_1,k_2,k_3)\,\delta_\text{D}^3(\bm{k}_1+\bm{k}_2+\bm{k}_3) = \langle\delta(\bm{k}_1)\delta(\bm{k}_2)\delta(\bm{k}_3)\rangle,
\end{equation}
which vanishes identically for a Gaussian field but carries additional cosmological information once non linearities become significant~\citep{sefu06}. In practice, however, this perturbative approach becomes rapidly more complex at higher orders, and even at next to leading order it already departs from full $N$-body simulation results by an order of magnitude on the mildly non linear scales relevant to this work~\citep{Chudaykin_2020}. This breakdown of analytic methods precisely on the scales that carry the most cosmological information, rather than any detailed treatment of perturbation theory itself, which is not the focus of this thesis, is the key motivation for turning to simulations, and ultimately to the machine learning based, field level approaches developed in later Chapters.

The large scale structure traced by all of these observables is, in short, the end product of a well understood but analytically intractable evolutionary history: a Gaussian random field of primordial perturbations, evolving under gravity into the highly non Gaussian cosmic web of halos, filaments, and voids described above~\citep[e.g.][]{obuljen23,villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}. As just shown, once gravitational (and, for baryonic tracers, astrophysical) non linearities become important, the power spectrum alone is no longer a complete statistical description. Information leaks into higher order correlations, and standard perturbative techniques used to model these statistics break down precisely on the mildly non linear and non linear scales that are often very important and information rich~\citep[e.g.][]{obuljen23}. Restricting analyses to the two point function alone therefore discards a substantial fraction of the cosmological information encoded in the field, particularly on the small scales probed by upcoming high resolution 21 cm IM and galaxy surveys.

This has motivated a shift, over the last several years, toward \emph{field level} inference and emulation. Rather than compressing a cosmological field into a small set of hand chosen summary statistics or before performing inference, one instead works directly with the field itself (or a learned representation of it), in principle retaining the full non Gaussian information content~\citep[e.g.][]{jamieson_field-level_2023,angulo_large-scale_2022}. An example in this direction is the work of \citet{rouhiainen_cosmology_2024}, which explicitly frames the non Gaussianity of the late-time, small scale LSS as the central obstacle to analytic modeling, and addresses it by learning the field level probability density of the matter distribution directly with normalizing flows , explicitly analogous to how gravitational evolution transforms an initially Gaussian field into today's complex non Gaussian one , before turning to denoising diffusion models for volumetric, 3D super resolution tasks. The approach taken in these work shares this field level philosophy. Rather than relying on summary statistics or hand calibrated prescriptions, we aim to learn the field level mapping between cosmological tracers directly from simulations, retaining small scale non Gaussian structure that summary statistic based or purely perturbative approaches would otherwise discard.

\subsection{Large-scale structure for cosmological parameter inference}
\label{sec:lss_param_inference}

The physics above directly shapes how the LSS is used in practice to constrain cosmological parameters such as $\Om$ and $\sige$. The traditional approach compresses an observed field (a galaxy catalogue, a weak lensing map, an intensity map) into a small set of hand chosen summary statistics, most commonly the power spectrum, and increasingly the bispectrum, and performs likelihood based (or Gaussian likelihood) inference on the resulting data vector, using perturbation theory or fitting functions such as HaloFit~\citep{Takahashi_2012} to model the observable as a function of $\bm{\phi}=(\Om,\sige,\dots)$. This approach is well understood and computationally cheap, but, as argued above, it is information lossy by construction on the mildly non linear and non linear scales where perturbation theory itself begins to fail~\citep[e.g.][]{Chudaykin_2020}.

Two developments over roughly the last decade have pushed beyond this. First, \emph{simulation based inference} (SBI, also a subset of a class of likelihood free inference) sidesteps the need for an analytic likelihood altogether. Cosmological simulations are run over a grid of parameters $\bm{\phi}$, and a learned surrogate (e.g.\ a neural density estimator) is trained on the resulting $(\bm{x},\bm{\phi})$ pairs to approximate the posterior $p(\bm{\phi}|\bm{x})$ directly~\citep[e.g.][]{jeffrey_likelihood-free_2020,angulo_large-scale_2022}. Large public simulation suites such as CAMELS~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_camels_2021} were built explicitly to enable this kind of approach across a wide range of both cosmological and astrophysical (feedback) parameters, and its associated Multifield Dataset~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_camels_2022} has become a standard benchmark for exactly this task. 

Second, and building on SBI, \emph{field level inference} removes the summary statistic compression step entirely, training models to infer $\bm{\phi}$ (or a full field level posterior) directly from the observed field itself, in principle maximizing the available cosmological information rather than only what survives compression into a power spectrum, bispectrum, or learned low dimensional summary~\citep[e.g.][]{jamieson_field-level_2023}.

Crucially, these methods are no longer confined to simulated forecasts. Field level and simulation based inference have already been applied directly to real observational data: a field level SBI analysis of the actual BOSS CMASS galaxy sample constrains $\sige$ to a precision approximately 2.65 times tighter than an equivalent power-spectrum-based analysis of the same data~\citep{lemos2023simbigfieldlevelsimulationbasedinference}, while a simulation-based, neural compression analysis of real Dark Energy Survey Year 3 weak lensing data constrains the dark energy equation of state parameter $w$ (under a $w$CDM model) more than twice as precisely as an equivalent power-spectrum-only analysis~\citep{jeffrey2024darkenergysurveyyear}. This demonstrates that simulation based and field level inference have moved from a promising forecasting tool to a method already delivering competitive cosmological constraints from real surveys.

This thesis adopts the same field level philosophy, but applied to a different pair of problems. Rather than only performing parameter inference from an observed field, we are also concerned with learning the field level \emph{mapping between cosmological tracers} (dark matter, halos, 21\,cm brightness temperature, baryonic fields) directly from simulations. The two goals are complementary. An accurate field level emulator of the kind developed in this thesis is itself a natural forward simulator for the SBI/field level parameter inference pipelines described above, since it can generate the large number of realistic, high resolution training pairs that such methods require far more cheaply than full hydrodynamical simulations, while capturing most, if not all of the correlations of the field at different spatial scales. 


\section{Cosmological Simulations}
\label{sec:cosmo_sims}

The analytic and semi analytic approaches discussed previously break down precisely in the non linear regime, and for baryons, around and below the Jeans scale, that carries the most information about structure formation. Numerically solving the coupled equations of gravity (and, where relevant, hydrodynamics) directly is the standard way around this limitation, and the simulations produced this way are also the training data for the machine learning models developed later in this thesis.

\subsection{N-body simulations}

The simplest class of cosmological simulation, an $N$-body simulation, discretizes the (collisionless) dark matter distribution into a large number of massive particles and evolves their positions and velocities forward in time under their mutual gravity, within a periodic comoving box that expands according to the background cosmology. Computing the gravitational force from every particle on every other particle exactly scales as $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$, which is prohibitive for the particle counts used in modern simulations; in practice, codes instead combine a hierarchical tree algorithm (accurate at short range) with a particle-mesh (PM) method solved via fast Fourier transforms (efficient at long range), an approach known as TreePM. \texttt{GADGET-4}~\citep{springel_simulating_2021} is a widely used, publicly available example of such a TreePM code, and (together with its predecessors) underlies many of the dark-matter-only simulations referenced throughout this thesis. The initial conditions for such a simulation are generated directly from the linear power spectrum and transfer function introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cosmological_context}, displaced onto the particle grid using a Lagrangian perturbation-theory scheme.

\subsection{Hydrodynamical simulations and subgrid physics}

Modeling baryonic tracers such as neutral hydrogen , rather than dark matter alone, requires simulating the gas dynamics as well as gravity. Two broad numerical strategies exist for this: smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH), which represents the gas as a set of particles whose properties are smoothed over a kernel, and the moving mesh approach, which instead discretizes the gas onto an unstructured, quasi-Lagrangian Voronoi mesh that moves with the flow. \texttt{Arepo}~\citep{weinberger_arepo_2020} is the moving-mesh code used by the IllustrisTNG simulations discussed below, and was developed in part to combine the adaptivity of SPH with the more accurate shock capturing and reduced numerical noise of a fixed-mesh (Eulerian) hydrodynamics solver. For the purposes of this thesis, \texttt{GADGET} and \texttt{Arepo} can both simply be thought of as the underlying numerical engines used to produce the simulations analyzed in later Chapters, differing in how they discretize the fluid.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

Even at the resolution of state of the art cosmological simulations, several physical processes central to galaxy formation, star formation, stellar and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback, gas cooling and heating and chemical enrichment, occur on scales far smaller than a single resolution element, and cannot be simulated from first principles. These processes are instead captured through effective \emph{subgrid} models: simplified prescriptions, calibrated against observations, that approximate the net effect of the unresolved physics on the resolved gas. This is directly relevant to this thesis's own results: the suppression of small scale power around $k\sim1\hMpci$ and the enhancement at $k\gg1\hMpci$ seen in hydrodynamical simulations relative to dark matter only ones (discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:ml_field_level}) originates precisely from these subgrid feedback processes, making them directly relevant to the fields this thesis aims to emulate.

For example,star formation is a useful, concrete example of how such a subgrid model works in practice. Following the widely used prescription of \citet{Springel_2003} (the basis for the star formation model in IllustrisTNG), gas above a threshold density is assumed to reside in a two phase interstellar medium, consisting of cold, dense clouds embedded in a hot ambient phase in local pressure equilibrium. The cold clouds are the sites of star formation. Rather than converting gas smoothly, individual gas elements above the density threshold are converted into collisionless star particles \emph{stochastically}, with a conversion probability, each timestep, set by a star formation timescale tuned so that the resulting population reproduces the observed relation between gas surface density and star-formation-rate surface density. Feedback, cooling, and chemical enrichment are treated with conceptually similar, but distinct, subgrid recipes, which are not detailed further here. The broader point is that not all small scale physics can be modeled with perfect physics based complexity, that would be computationally prohibitive. This introduces a fundamental limitation: subgrid approximations introduce systematic uncertainties and biases into the simulated fields. Consequently, any machine learning model trained on these simulations inherits these limitations as an upper bound on reconstruction accuracy. No matter how well our inference, reconstruction or generation method performs, it cannot surpass the fidelity of the ground truth simulation data on which it was trained.

\subsection{The IllustrisTNG simulations}

IllustrisTNG (TNG) is a suite of large volume, high resolution magnetohydrodynamical simulations run with \texttt{Arepo}, incorporating a comprehensive galaxy-formation model built on subgrid prescriptions of the kind described above~\citep{nelson2021illustristngsimulationspublicdata,Pillepich_2017}. Multiple box sizes and resolutions are publicly available (TNG50, TNG100, TNG300), trading volume against resolution; the TNG300 and TNG100 volumes, whose computational cost is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:sim_bottleneck}, are the ones directly used in this thesis. What distinguishes TNG from a dark-matter-only simulation of the same volume is precisely the addition of gas dynamics and the calibrated subgrid physics described above, which is what allows TNG to produce realistic galaxy populations, and, of direct relevance to this thesis, physically motivated baryonic fields (gas temperature, HI content, stellar density) alongside the dark matter distribution.

\subsection{CAMELS}

The Cosmology and Astrophysics with MachinE Learning Simulations (CAMELS) project~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_camels_2021} takes a complementary approach: rather than a small number of very large, fixed-cosmology volumes, CAMELS runs thousands of smaller simulations, varying \emph{both} cosmological parameters (e.g.\ $\Om$, $\sige$) \emph{and} astrophysical/subgrid feedback parameters across a broad range, using multiple independent simulation codes and galaxy-formation models. This was explicitly designed to enable machine learning and simulation-based inference applications that need to be trained , and, critically, tested for robustness , across the full space of plausible feedback scenarios, not just cosmological parameters, since real feedback physics is itself uncertain. This robustness-to astrophysics concern is directly relevant to any machine learning emulator built on a single simulation suite (such as the TNG-based methods in this thesis), and is revisited in the discussion of future work.

\subsection{The Lyman-alpha forest}

A different, complementary probe of the diffuse intergalactic medium (IGM) , as opposed to the collapsed halos traced by HI intensity mapping, or the luminous galaxies traced by the surveys discussed in Section~\ref{sec:galaxy_surveys} , is provided by the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest: a dense series of absorption features imprinted on the spectra of high redshift background quasars by intervening neutral hydrogen along the line of sight.

The standard way to model this signal in a simulation is to extract one dimensional \emph{line of sight (LOS) skewers}: the gas density, temperature, and peculiar velocity are read off along a sightline through the simulated volume, mimicking the path a real quasar's light would take through the intervening IGM. Each skewer is then converted into a mock absorption (transmitted flux) spectrum via an integral of the local optical depth along the line of sight, using the same self shielding and optical-depth machinery introduced in Section~\ref{sec:21cm_im} for the 21\,cm signal. Crucially, the resulting flux spectrum is not a simple tracer of the dark matter density alone: it depends on the baryonic gas density, on its thermal state (through the IGM temperature,density relation, itself shaped by the same feedback and heating processes discussed above), and on the line of sight peculiar velocity field, which distorts the mapping between real space and the observed (velocity/redshift) space of the spectrum. This sensitivity to genuinely baryonic physics , and not just to the underlying gravitational clustering , is precisely why hydrodynamical, rather than dark-matter-only, simulations are required to model the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest realistically, echoing the Jeans-scale argument made in Section~\ref{sec:cosmological_context}.

The Lyman-$\alpha$ analysis in this thesis (Chapter~\ref{ch:dps}) is built on the Sherwood-Relics suite of hydrodynamical simulations~\citep{bolton_sherwood_2017,puchwein_sherwood-relics_2023}, run with \texttt{GADGET-3} at $z=2.4$ in periodic boxes of comoving side $40\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}$. This is the same simulation suite used by the variational auto encoder(VAE)-based DeepCHART reconstruction method~\citep{Maitra_2026}, adopted here for direct comparability with that closest piece of related field level machine learning work. (Chapter~\ref{ch:dps}'s own introduction, Section~\ref{sec:dps_intro}, reviews this and semi-analytic approaches such as TARDIS~\citep{horowitz_2019,horowitz_2021}, in more detail.) The resulting posterior reconstructions, evaluated through the line of sight power spectrum of each recovered field and through Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients against the ground truth, across varying sightline sampling densities and flux signal to noise ratios, are presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:dps} (Section~\ref{sec:dps_results}).

\section{21cm Cosmology and Intensity Mapping}
\label{sec:21cm_im}

\subsection{The hyperfine transition and spin temperature}

Neutral hydrogen (HI) has a ground-state hyperfine splitting, caused by the interaction between the magnetic moments of the proton and electron, whose transition corresponds to a rest-frame wavelength of 21\,cm (frequency $\nu_{21}=1420.4$\,MHz). The relative population of the two hyperfine levels is described by the spin temperature $T_S$, defined through the Boltzmann relation between the level populations,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{n_1}{n_0} = 3\,e^{-T_\star/T_S}
\end{equation}
where $n_1$ and $n_0$ are the populations of the upper and lower hyperfine levels, the factor of 3 is the ratio of their statistical weights, and $T_\star\equiv h\nu_{21}/k_B\approx0.068$\,K is the temperature corresponding to the transition energy.

The spin temperature is set by a competition between three effects: absorption/stimulated emission of CMB photons, which drives $T_S$ towards the CMB temperature $T_\text{CMB}$; collisions between hydrogen atoms (and, at high redshift, with free electrons), which drive $T_S$ towards the kinetic temperature of the gas $T_K$; and scattering of Lyman-$\alpha$ photons, which does the same via the Wouthuysen,Field effect~\citep{Furlanetto:2006}. In the regimes relevant to this thesis, at the redshifts and densities probed by post-reionization 21\,cm intensity mapping, collisions dominate and are efficient enough that $T_S$ closely tracks $T_K$, decoupling it from $T_\text{CMB}$.

The observable 21\,cm signal is the differential brightness temperature of a patch of sky relative to the CMB, which can be written schematically as
\begin{equation}
    \delta T_b(z) \approx \bar{T}_b(z)\,\big(1+\delta_\text{HI}\big)\left(1 - \frac{T_\text{CMB}}{T_S}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\delta_\text{HI}$ is the HI density contrast and $\bar{T}_b(z)$ is a redshift-dependent normalization set by the mean HI abundance and the background cosmology~\citep{Furlanetto:2006}. In the post-reionization regime relevant to the intensity mapping experiments discussed in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, $T_S\gg T_\text{CMB}$ to good approximation, so the factor in parentheses tends to unity and the 21\,cm signal is seen in emission, tracing $(1+\delta_\text{HI})$ directly.

\subsection{self shielding and optical depth}

Not all neutral hydrogen contributes equally to the observable signal: in the presence of an ionizing ultraviolet background, gas at low density is highly
ionized, while sufficiently dense gas self shields against this background and remains predominantly neutral. This transition is governed by the local optical depth to ionizing photons, and is commonly modeled in simulations via the fitting formula of~\citet{Rahmati_2013}, which prescribes the local
photoionization rate as a function of gas density:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{phot}}}{\Gamma_{\mathrm{UVB}}}
    = (1 - f)\!\left[1 + \left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{H}}}{n_0}\right)^{\!\beta}\right]^{\alpha_1}
    + f\!\left[1 + \frac{n_{\mathrm{H}}}{n_0}\right]^{\alpha_2},
    \label{eq:rahmati}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{UVB}}$ is the uniform UV background rate, $n_0$ is a characteristic self shielding density threshold that depends on redshift and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{UVB}}$, and $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, $\beta$, $f$
are parameters calibrated against full radiative transfer simulations. At low densities ($n_{\mathrm{H}} \ll n_0$), the gas sees the full background and is highly ionized; at high densities ($n_{\mathrm{H}} \gg n_0$), $\Gamma_{\mathrm{phot}}$ is strongly suppressed and the gas remains predominantly neutral. The neutral fraction itself is then obtained by solving the ionization equilibrium between photoionization, collisional ionization, and recombination at each resolution element. This self shielding prescription is
what allows a simulation, which by default tracks total gas (and dark matter) density, to be converted into a physically meaningful HI field for comparison
with 21\,cm observations, rather than simply assuming all gas is neutral.

At the level of the observed signal itself, the post-reionization 21\,cm line is generally an optically thin tracer: the probability that an emitted photon is re-absorbed before escaping is small, so the simple, linear relation between $\delta T_b$ and $\delta_\text{HI}$ above holds without needing full radiative transfer. This is a convenient simplification exploited throughout the modeling approaches discussed below, though it is worth noting explicitly since it is not guaranteed to hold in all regimes (e.g.\ deep inside very dense, self shielded structures).

\subsection{Intensity mapping}

Rather than individually detecting and cataloguing galaxies, intensity mapping (IM) measures the aggregate, unresolved 21\,cm emission from all of the HI within a given angular pixel and frequency channel, effectively treating the sky as a continuous 3D field rather than a catalogue of discrete sources. This has two main advantages over traditional galaxy redshift surveys: it does not require individually resolving faint sources, allowing much larger comoving volumes and higher redshifts to be surveyed in a given observing time~\citep{Villaescusa-Navarro:2018}; and the observed frequency directly encodes the redshift of the emitting gas via the cosmological redshifting of the 21\,cm line, providing three dimensional maps without the need for spectroscopic follow up of individual objects~\citep[e.g.][]{Bharadwaj:2000,Battye:2004,Chang:2007}. The price paid for this efficiency is that the signal is dominated by bright astrophysical and instrumental foregrounds (Galactic synchrotron emission, extragalactic point sources, and radio-frequency interference) that must be modeled and removed, and that individual objects can no longer be identified , only the aggregate field. Cross-correlating IM maps with an independent, overlapping galaxy survey is a standard and powerful strategy for both validating the recovered HI signal and mitigating foreground contamination, since foregrounds are not expected to correlate with the (independently observed) galaxy positions.

\subsection{Modeling the HI field}

Translating between the underlying matter distribution and the observable HI field that intensity mapping probes requires a model connecting the two, and three broad approaches exist.

\paragraph{Halo models.} Halo-model approaches assign HI to dark matter halos according to a prescribed HI,halo mass relation $M_\text{HI}(M)$~\citep{padmanabhan_hi_2023}, often calibrated against observations of damped Lyman-$\alpha$ systems, both from the halo-model side~\citep{Padmanabhan_2016} and observationally~\citep{Dev_2023,Obuljen_2019}. This approach is computationally cheap and can be applied to very large volumes, but relies on a simplified, effective description of a process that is, in reality, governed by detailed gas physics within and around individual halos.

\paragraph{Perturbation-theory-based modeling.} As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cosmological_context}, standard perturbation theory can in principle be extended to describe biased tracers such as HI~\citep{obuljen23}, but is subject to the same breakdown on mildly non linear and non linear scales as the dark matter perturbation theory discussed there, and is not treated in further analytic detail in this thesis.

\paragraph{Hydrodynamical simulations.} Full hydrodynamical simulations solve for the gas dynamics directly (subject to the self shielding and other subgrid prescriptions discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cosmo_sims}), capturing small scale astrophysical processes , star formation, feedback, radiative transfer , that the two approaches above must instead approximate~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}. This comes at substantially higher computational cost, as quantified in Section~\ref{sec:sim_bottleneck}, and is the primary motivation, throughout this thesis, for developing fast machine learning based surrogates that retain hydrodynamical simulation fidelity at a fraction of the cost.

Beyond these three established approaches, a growing body of work instead learns the HI,matter mapping directly from simulations in a data driven way, without prescribing a specific functional form for $M_\text{HI}(M)$ in advance~\citep[e.g.][]{hitz2025fastsimulationcosmologicalneutral,li2022theoreticalmodelsatomichydrogen,spinelli_atomic_2020}. These forward modeling approaches extend predictions to large volumes by applying calibrated prescriptions for the HI,halo connection, but still fall short of learning the full field level mapping from simulations , the specific gap that the methods developed in this thesis address.

\section{Galaxy Surveys}
\label{sec:galaxy_surveys}

Alongside 21\,cm intensity mapping, galaxy surveys remain the most established observational route to the large scale structure, and it is worth briefly reviewing the two dominant approaches , spectroscopic redshift surveys and weak gravitational lensing , both to contextualize 21\,cm IM among the broader observational landscape and because cross-correlation with these surveys is a recurring theme, and an explicit long-term goal, of this thesis.

\subsection{Redshift surveys and baryon acoustic oscillations}

Spectroscopic redshift surveys measure the three dimensional positions of large numbers of galaxies by combining their angular position on the sky with a redshift obtained from an optical spectrum, directly mapping out the galaxy distribution as a tracer of the underlying matter field. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) and its successors the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS)~\citep{dawson_baryon_2013,BOSS:2016} and its extension eBOSS have produced some of the most precise galaxy clustering measurements to date, and the ongoing Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey is extending this approach to substantially larger volumes and higher redshifts~\citep{DESIde}.

A key feature exploited by these surveys is the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) signal: acoustic waves in the pre recombination photon-baryon plasma imprint a characteristic scale on the late-time galaxy distribution, corresponding to the sound horizon at recombination. Because this physical scale is known precisely from CMB physics, its apparent size in a galaxy survey acts as a ``standard ruler'', providing a geometric measurement of the expansion history that is largely insensitive to the details of non linear structure formation , in contrast to the shape of the full power spectrum, which is sensitive to precisely the non linear physics discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cosmological_context}. A second, complementary signal available in the same data is redshift-space distortions (RSD): peculiar velocities along the line of sight distort the observed (redshift-space) galaxy positions relative to their true, real-space positions, providing a measurement of the growth rate of structure and thus an additional handle on gravity and the growth factor $D_+(a)$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cosmological_context}~\citep[e.g.][]{Ross:2014}.

\subsection{Weak gravitational lensing}

A complementary approach, sensitive to the total matter distribution (dark and baryonic) rather than only to the (biased) tracer traced by galaxy positions, is weak gravitational lensing. Light from distant background galaxies is very slightly deflected and sheared by the intervening large scale structure along the line of sight; while this distortion is far too small to detect in any single galaxy image, it can be measured statistically from the coherent alignment of many background galaxy shapes, a signal known as cosmic shear. Major ongoing and recent lensing surveys include the Dark Energy Survey (DES)~\citep{abbott:2018} and the Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS)~\citep{Hildebrandt:2017}, with substantially larger and deeper surveys upcoming: Euclid~\citep{Euclid:2019clj,euclid_collaboration_et_al_euclid_2020,scaramella_euclid_2022} and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST)~\citep{Ivezic_2019}.

Because weak lensing probes the total matter distribution directly, rather than a specific luminous or 21\,cm tracer, cross-correlating a lensing survey with 21\,cm intensity maps (or with any of the tracers discussed elsewhere in this Chapter) provides a way to isolate genuine cosmological signal from tracer-specific systematics and astrophysical uncertainties that do not correlate between independent probes , directly analogous to the galaxy,IM cross-correlation strategy already discussed for foreground mitigation. Cross-correlations between weak lensing and post-reionization neutral hydrogen probes have already been explored as a route to constraining dark energy~\citep{Dash:2020}, and this kind of multi-tracer cross-correlation, extended to the field level using the machine learning tools developed later in this thesis, is precisely the long-term goal motivating the joint dark-matter-and-halo, and dark-matter-and-baryonic-field, emulation approaches presented in the following Chapters.

\section{Current and Upcoming Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The theoretical picture developed above is being tested by a rapidly growing set of 21\,cm intensity mapping experiments, ranging from currently operating pathfinders to next generation facilities still under construction.

\subsection{21cm intensity mapping instruments}

The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) is a transit interferometer without moving parts, covering the frequency range corresponding to $0.8\lesssim z\lesssim2.5$~\citep{Bandura:2014gwa}; CHIME has already reported a direct detection of cosmological 21\,cm emission~\citep{CHIMEdetection}, a major milestone for interferometric 21\,cm IM. Related interferometric instruments include CHORD~\citep{thechordcollaboration2026overviewcanadianhydrogenobservatory}, a next generation Canadian array currently under construction and designed to substantially improve on CHIME's sensitivity and frequency coverage, and HIRAX, a planned array of $\sim$1000 six-metre dishes to be sited in South Africa, targeting baryon acoustic oscillations over a wide sky area and redshift range $0.8\lesssim z\lesssim2.5$~\citep{Newburgh:2016mwi}; as of this writing, HIRAX remains in the dish deployment and construction phase, with cosmological results still forthcoming. On the single-dish side, the Green Bank Telescope (GBT) was used for some of the earliest cross-correlation detections of the 21\,cm signal~\citep{Masui2013,Wolz2022}, and the Five-hundred-metre Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) in China, the largest single-dish radio telescope in the world, is forecast to deliver competitive intensity mapping constraints once dedicated IM surveys are carried out~\citep{Hu:2019okh}.

\subsection{MeerKAT and MeerKLASS}

MeerKAT, a 64-dish interferometer in South Africa and an SKA precursor, has become one of the most productive current facilities for single-dish 21\,cm intensity mapping through its MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey (MeerKLASS) programme~\citep{Santos:2017,meerklass_deepfield_2025}. Substantial effort has gone into the calibration and foreground-mitigation pipeline needed to extract a cosmological signal from single-dish autocorrelation data~\citep{Wang:2021,irfan2022}, culminating in a first cross-correlation detection of the 21\,cm signal with WiggleZ galaxies at $7.7\sigma$ significance~\citep{Cunnington:2022uzo}. More recently, deeper MeerKLASS observations, cross-correlated with an independent spectroscopic galaxy sample, have pushed this kind of measurement to greater statistical significance and to new redshift ranges, representing the deepest single-dish HI intensity maps obtained to date. Taken together, these results mark a genuine milestone for the field, demonstrating that single-dish 21\,cm intensity mapping can deliver robust cosmological cross-correlation detections ahead of the full SKA era.

\subsection{The Square Kilometre Array Observatory}

Radio cosmology is also one of the flagship science drivers of the Square Kilometre Array Observatory (SKAO),\footnote{\url{https://www.skao.int/}} which will ultimately comprise two complementary telescopes: SKA-Low, located in Western Australia and optimized for lower radio frequencies (and correspondingly higher redshifts, probing the epoch of reionization and beyond), and SKA-Mid, located in South Africa (at the same site as MeerKAT, which will be incorporated into the array), covering higher frequencies corresponding to the lower-redshift, post-reionization Universe. Operated in single-dish (autocorrelation) mode, SKA-Mid is expected to conduct 21\,cm intensity mapping surveys across cosmologically relevant volumes out to $z\sim3$~\citep{Santos:2015,Bacon:2018}, directly extending the redshift range already being explored by its MeerKAT/MeerKLASS precursor. Both SKA-Low and SKA-Mid are currently under construction, with phased science operations expected to begin as construction and commissioning progress; the results already obtained by CHIME and MeerKAT/MeerKLASS, discussed above, serve as important pathfinders and proofs of concept for the much larger surveys the SKAO is ultimately designed to carry out.

Finally, it is worth noting that several of the galaxy-survey instruments already discussed in Section~\ref{sec:galaxy_surveys} (DESI, Euclid, LSST/Rubin) are contemporaries of these 21\,cm facilities, and are expected to substantially overlap with them in sky coverage and observing epoch, further motivating the kind of cross-correlation and multi-tracer approaches that this thesis is ultimately aimed at supporting.

\section{From Landscape to Bottleneck}

The preceding sections have laid out, in turn, the theoretical machinery used to describe the cosmological matter and HI fields, the simulation methods needed once that modeling moves beyond the reach of linear theory and perturbation theory, the physics of the 21\,cm signal itself and the modeling strategies used to predict it, the complementary galaxy-survey probes that trace the same large scale structure through independent tracers, and the current and upcoming instruments , from CHIME and MeerKAT/MeerKLASS to the SKAO , that are now turning all of this theoretical machinery into an observational programme.

A common thread runs through this landscape: in every case, the most physically complete description , full hydrodynamical simulations, resolving gravity, gas dynamics, and the subgrid astrophysics of star formation and feedback , is also the most computationally expensive one, precisely in the small scale, non linear regime ($k\gtrsim k_{\rm NL}$) where the wealth of new instruments described above is beginning to deliver data. Perturbative and halo-based approaches remain analytically tractable in this regime, but only by trading away exactly the physical content , baryonic feedback, self shielded HI, small scale non linear clustering , that upcoming surveys are most sensitive to. This tension, between the fidelity of hydrodynamical simulations and their cost at the scale required for modern inference, is the subject of the following section.

\section{The Simulation Bottleneck}
\label{sec:sim_bottleneck}

As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cosmo_sims}, cosmological simulations are essential tools for understanding the formation and evolution of structure in the Universe, letting us explore the complex interplay of gravitational, hydrodynamic, and astrophysical processes shaping the distribution of matter from large down to highly non linear galactic and sub-galactic scales. However, high fidelity hydrodynamical simulations are computationally very expensive, limiting their applicability when the parameter space is large in inference studies. For example, the IllustrisTNG simulations~\citep{nelson2021illustristngsimulationspublicdata,Pillepich_2017} required approximately 35 million CPU-hours for the largest \TNG run and about 18 million CPU-hours for TNG100, while EAGLE~\citep{EAGLE} consumed roughly 4.5 million CPU-hours.

Such computational demands become particularly prohibitive for cosmological or astrophysical field level inference and simulation-based inference (SBI) frameworks, which require large ensembles of conditional forward simulations to explore the parameter spaces. Recent works have shown promising applications, including the cosmological context~\citep{angulo_large-scale_2022,jeffrey_likelihood-free_2020,bairagi2025simulationsneedsimulationbasedinference,Zeghal_2025,karchev2023simsimssimulationbasedsupernovaia,Saxena_2024,tucci24}. However, their applicability remains limited by the requirement of generating sufficient high fidelity simulations. This computational bottleneck underlines the need for fast, accurate surrogate models capable of reproducing cosmological fields at a fraction of the cost , not limited to dark matter, but crucially including baryonic observables that can be directly constrained with data.

This same bottleneck also constrains theoretical modeling of the 21 cm signal itself, which currently follows three main approaches: halo models (see~\citet{padmanabhan_hi_2023}), perturbation theory (e.g.~\citep{obuljen23}), or hydrodynamical simulations of structure formation incorporating the relevant physical processes (e.g.~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}). Each of these methods has both advantages and disadvantages. For example, modeling the IM signal requires both large volumes and high resolution to fully resolve the physics of the small mass ($\sim$ 10$^{10}$ M$_{\odot}/h$) dark matter halos that host HI, something that is difficult to achieve with full hydrodynamical simulations due to the range of scales involved. In the context of halo models, extensive work has been expended to study the mass limit for halos to retain HI, for example by looking at the damped Lyman-$\alpha$ systems statistics, both from the point of view of halo models~\citep{Padmanabhan_2016} and observationally~\citep{Dev_2023,Obuljen_2019}. In addition to these semi-analytical and perturbative frameworks, several forward modeling approaches have been developed to predict the HI field directly from dark-matter simulations by prescribing or calibrating the $M_{\rm HI}(M)$ relation and other parameters on observations or hydrodynamical runs~\citep[e.g.][]{hitz2025fastsimulationcosmologicalneutral,li2022theoreticalmodelsatomichydrogen,spinelli_atomic_2020}. These methods, however, extend predictions to large volumes by applying calibrated prescriptions for the HI,halo connection, rather than learning the full field level mapping from simulations. Thus, a method capable of learning the small scale physics from state of the art simulations while at the same time reaching large scales and volumes would be of great importance , this is the computational and modeling gap this thesis addresses.

\section{Bayesian Inference Basics}
\label{sec:bayesian_basics}

Cosmology is, in a specific sense, harder than most experimental sciences: we only ever get to observe one Universe, at one set of times, from one vantage point. There is no way to re-run the experiment with a different $\Om$ or $\sige$ and see what comes out. This makes the Bayesian interpretation of probability , as a degree of belief, updated in light of data, rather than a long-run frequency of repeated trials , the natural framework for cosmological inference, and it is the language used throughout the following Chapters (in particular, the external-prior combination in Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos} relies directly on it).

Bayes' theorem itself follows from a basic property of joint probabilities, $p(\theta,\bm{d}) = p(\bm{d}|\theta)\,p(\theta) = p(\theta|\bm{d})\,p(\bm{d})$, rearranged to read
\begin{equation}
    p(\theta\,|\,\bm{d}) = \frac{p(\bm{d}\,|\,\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(\bm{d})},
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the parameters of interest (e.g.\ $\Om,\sige$) and $\bm{d}$ the observed data. Each term has a name and a role:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $p(\theta)$, the \emph{prior}, encodes what is known (or assumed) about $\theta$ before seeing the data at hand , e.g.\ a previous CMB constraint, or simply a broad, uninformative range.
    \item $p(\bm{d}\,|\,\theta)$, the \emph{likelihood}, is the forward model: given a value of $\theta$, how probable is it to observe this particular data $\bm{d}$? This is where a physical model of the observable enters, whether that model is an explicit Gaussian likelihood on a summary statistic, or (as in Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator}) an implicit one defined only through forward simulations.
    \item $p(\theta\,|\,\bm{d})$, the \emph{posterior}, is the object of interest: the updated belief about $\theta$ once the data $\bm{d}$ has been taken into account, combining prior knowledge with what the data itself says.
    \item $p(\bm{d})$, the \emph{evidence}, is a normalization constant (the probability of the data, marginalized over all possible $\theta$) that does not need to be computed for parameter inference, but becomes important when comparing two different models rather than two values of the same parameter.
\end{itemize}

The whole point of an inference pipeline, in this language, is to get from $p(\theta)$ to $p(\theta|\bm{d})$: to turn a prior belief into a posterior one, using the data through the likelihood. Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos} does this in a specific and unusual way, folding an external, already-existing cosmological posterior (from Planck) into a learned generative model as an additional prior at inference time, without having to retrain the underlying network for every new prior one might want to impose , an application of exactly the identity above.

\section{Machine Learning for Field Level Cosmology}
\label{sec:ml_field_level}

In recent years, machine learning techniques, and in particular generative models, have emerged as powerful emulators to reproduce cosmological and astrophysical fields~\citep{perraudin2019cosmologicalnbodysimulationschallenge,Li_2021,jamieson_field-level_2023,Rigo_2025,hassan_hiflow_2022,andrianomena_emulating_2022,sharma25,bernardini2025ember2,andrianomena2024cosmologicalmultifieldemulator}. Among them, \emph{diffusion models} have emerged as the current state of the art for high fidelity generative modeling, and are the central tool used throughout this thesis. Given their importance to everything that follows, we introduce them here in some detail, together with the surrounding building blocks, convolutional networks, attention, normalizing flows, and simulation-based inference , that the rest of the thesis assumes some familiarity with.

\subsection{Convolutional neural networks and the U-Net}
\label{sec:cnn_unet}

Most of the fields this thesis works with, namely dark matter density, HI
brightness temperature, gas temperature and HI ionization fraction, are naturally represented as 2D or
3D grids of voxel values, and the mappings we want to learn (dark matter to
halos, dark matter to gas temperature, and so on) is local in a specific
sense: the value of the target field at a given voxel to a good approximation depends on the
source field in a neighborhood around that voxel, not on the field far
away. A \emph{convolutional neural network} (CNN) is built around exactly
this assumption. Rather than connecting every input voxel to every output
voxel with an independent weight, as a generic fully-connected network
would, a convolutional layer instead slides a small, learnable filter, or
\emph{kernel}, typically a few voxels across, over the input field,
computing a weighted sum of the local neighborhood at every position (see
the bottom panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:CNN}). Formally, for a single-channel
input $x$ and a $K\times K$ kernel $w$ with bias $b$, the value of the
output feature map at position $(i,j)$ is

\begin{equation}
    y_{i,j} = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{K}\sum_{n=1}^{K} w_{m,n}\,
    x_{i+m,\,j+n} \; + \; b\right),
    \label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is a non linear activation function, discussed below. For an
input of spatial size $N\times N$, kernel size $K$, stride $s$, and
zero-padding $p$, the resulting output has size

\begin{equation}
    N_{\rm out} = \left\lfloor \frac{N - K + 2p}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 ,
    \label{eq:output_size}
\end{equation}

so that, e.g., a $7\times7$ input convolved with a $3\times3$ kernel at
stride $1$ and no padding yields a $5\times5$ output, as shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:CNN}. The same filter weights $w_{m,n}$ are reused at every
position (``weight sharing''), which both drastically reduces the number of
parameters to learn and builds in translation invariance: a feature the
network learns to detect (e.g.\ a halo-scale overdensity) is detected the
same way wherever it appears in the box.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{chapters//ch0_introduction/CNN.pdf}
    \caption{Top: a canonical convolutional network, where the input is
    progressively transformed through convolution, non linear activation,
    and pooling layers into a compact set of deep features, before being
    combined by a final fully-connected layer, here shown for an image
    classification task, though the same building blocks apply equally to
    the field to field regression networks used throughout this thesis.
    Bottom: detail of a single $3\times3$ convolutional kernel $W$, acting
    on an input of shape $7\times7$ to produce a $5\times5$ output (dashed
    vs.\ solid outline indicating adjacent kernel positions at stride~1).}
    \label{fig:CNN}
\end{figure}

The non linear activation $\sigma$ applied after each convolution is what
allows the network to represent non linear relationships; throughout this
thesis it is almost always either a rectified linear unit (ReLU),

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x),
    \label{eq:relu}
\end{equation}
 or a Sigmoid Linear Unit(SiLU)

 \begin{equation}
\text{SiLU}(x) = x \cdot \sigma(x) = \frac{x}{1 + e^{-x}},
\label{eq:silu}
\end{equation}

Stacking several convolutional layers, usually interleaved with such an
activation and occasionally a downsampling (pooling) step, lets the network
build up increasingly large effective receptive fields and increasingly
abstract features from small scale texture in the first layers to
large-scale structure in the deeper ones. For max-pooling with a
$2\times2$ window and stride $2$, as shown in the top panel of
Fig.~\ref{fig:CNN},

\begin{equation}
    y_{i,j} = \max_{(m,n)\,\in\,\{0,1\}^2} x_{2i+m,\,2j+n} ,
    \label{eq:maxpool}
\end{equation}

which halves the spatial resolution while retaining the largest value in each $2\times2$ block. In a classification setting (for example figuring out the hand written numbers between 0 -9) the final
feature maps would be flattened and passed through a fully-connected layer
and a softmax to assign class probabilities (tell us the probability pf what the network thinks is the class the input belongs to), as in Fig.~\ref{fig:CNN}. However the
field to field networks used in this thesis instead keep a spatial output
at every stage, as described next.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{chapters//ch0_introduction/Unet.pdf}
    \caption{The network architecture of a standard U-net. This particular Unet works with 2D images. The dimension of the latent space at each level is mentioned, with $C$ being the number of feature channels learnt by the Unet.}
    \label{fig:unet}
\end{figure}
A \emph{U-Net}~\citep{ronneberger_u-net_2015} (See Fig~\ref{fig:unet}) is a specific, and by now
very standard, way of arranging convolutional layers for tasks where the
input and output are both fields of the same kind (an ``image-to image'',
or here ``field to field'', translation task): an \emph{encoder} path of
convolutional layers progressively downsamples the input into a coarser,
more abstract representation, a \emph{decoder} path then progressively
upsamples this representation back up to the original resolution, and
\emph{skip connections} directly link each encoder layer to its
corresponding decoder layer at the same resolution. In the specific image we show, the Unet is designed to work with 2 dimensional images. Each convolution block generally consists of 2 convolution layers, with a convolution layer depicted in the bottom image of Fig~\ref{fig:CNN}. Each subsequent layer reduces the dimension of the data while adding more channels to them. Each channel represents an extracted feature of the data, that is learnt by the network. The skip connections are what make the U-Net well suited to field level cosmology specifically:
without them, the fine, small scale detail present in the input (which the
encoder discards on its way to the coarse bottleneck representation) would
simply be lost, and the decoder would have to reconstruct it from scratch;
with them, that detail is carried directly across the network and only
needs to be combined with the new information the network has learned,
rather than regenerated. Every network used to map between fields in this
thesis , the dark-matter-to halo network of Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi}, the
denoising networks underlying the diffusion models of
Chapters~\ref{ch:lodi} and~\ref{ch:cosmofold}, and the galaxy/HI emulator
of Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator} is a variant of this same U-Net design.

Within each stage of a U-Net, and more generally, in most sufficiently
deep convolutional networks, individual convolutional layers are
typically grouped into \emph{residual blocks}~\citep{he_deep_2015} rather
than stacked directly. A residual block (Fig.~\ref{fig:resnet_block} computes some transformation
$g(\bm{x})$ of its input (e.g.\ two convolutional layers with
normalization and a non linearity in between) and then adds the original
input back, $\bm{x} + g(\bm{x})$, rather than outputting $g(\bm{x})$ alone.
This is a different kind of skip connection from the encoder-to decoder
ones described above, a much shorter-range one, spanning only a single
block rather than the whole network, but it serves a related purpose: it
lets the network default to the identity mapping if a given block turns
out not to be useful, which in practice makes much deeper networks
trainable, since gradients can flow backward through the addition even if
the learned transformation $g$ itself is poorly conditioned. Every U-Net
used in this thesis is built from stacks of such residual blocks, not from
bare convolutional layers.

These networks are also, almost without exception, not unconditional: they
need to be told about additional information beyond the input field itself, a cosmological parameter, an astrophysical feedback strength parameter, a
diffusion timestep, or several of these at once. The simplest way to do
this is to concatenate the conditioning information as extra input
channels, which is what the diffusion models of Chapters~\ref{ch:lodi}
and~\ref{ch:cosmofold} do (Section~\ref{sec:diffusion_intro}). A more
flexible alternative, used for the multi-parameter conditioning of
Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator}, is \emph{feature-wise linear modulation}
(FiLM)~\citep{perez2017filmvisualreasoninggeneral}: rather than adding the
conditioning information as an input, a small auxiliary network maps it
onto a pair of vectors $\boldsymbol\gamma,\boldsymbol\beta$, one value per
feature channel, which then rescale and shift every channel of an
intermediate feature map,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FiLM}(\bm{x},\bm{c}) = \boldsymbol\gamma(\bm{c})\odot\bm{x} + \boldsymbol\beta(\bm{c}).
    \label{eq:film}
\end{equation}
Because this modulation is applied at every layer rather than only at the
input, it gives the conditioning information many more opportunities to
influence the network's computation, which matters when, as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator}, the network needs to generalize across a
continuous range of several conditioning parameters at once rather than
being retrained for each one.

% [OPTION B: relocated from sec:lss_param_inference, with a new lead-in clause]
This kind of convolutional field to parameter mapping has already been benchmarked directly on the CAMELS Multifield Dataset introduced in Section~\ref{sec:lss_param_inference}: convolutional networks trained on CAMELS multifield maps can already extract $\Om$ and $\sige$ while marginalizing over four independent supernova and AGN feedback parameters~\citep{villaescusanavarro2021multifieldcosmologyartificialintelligence}, and graph neural networks operating directly on galaxy or halo point clouds achieve comparable results, inferring $\Om$ to within roughly 12\% from as few as $\sim$1000 galaxy positions in a single small simulation volume~\citep{Villanueva_Domingo_2022}.

\subsection{Attention}
\label{sec:attention}

A convolutional layer, by construction, only looks at a small local
neighborhood at a time; a feature at one corner of the box can only
influence a feature at the opposite corner once information has passed
through enough stacked convolutional layers to connect the two, which can
require many layers and does not always happen efficiently in practice.
This is a real limitation for cosmological fields, where genuinely
long-range effects exist large scale gravitational fields, or
feedback from a single active galactic nucleus displacing gas well beyond
its host halo and a network that can only combine nearby voxels may
struggle to capture them.

\emph{self attention}~\citep{vaswani_attention_2023} addresses this by
letting every position in a feature map directly interact with every other
position, regardless of distance (Fig.~\ref{fig:attention}). Concretely,
given a set of $n$ input token (voxel) embeddings stacked into a matrix
$X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$, three learned linear projections map $X$ onto
a set of \emph{queries}, \emph{keys}, and \emph{values},

\begin{equation}
    Q = XW_Q, \qquad K = XW_K, \qquad V = XW_V,
    \label{eq:qkv}
\end{equation}

with $Q,K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_v}$.
The similarity between every pair of positions is then obtained from the
dot product of their query and key vectors, $QK^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times
n}$, rescaled by $\sqrt{d_k}$ to keep the resulting values in a numerically
well-behaved range, and passed through a row-wise softmax so that the
weights attributed by any one position to all others sum to one,

\begin{equation}
    A = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right),
    \label{eq:attention_weights}
\end{equation}

giving an $n\times n$ matrix of \emph{attention weights}. The final,
context-aware output is a weighted sum of all positions' value vectors,
using these weights,

\begin{equation}
    Z = AV = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
    \label{eq:attention_output}
\end{equation}

with $Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_v}$. In effect, every voxel gets to ``ask''
the rest of the field, through its query, which other voxels' keys are
most relevant to it, and then assembles its output from exactly those
voxels' values , rather than being restricted to a fixed local window, as
a convolution is.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{chapters//ch0_introduction/Attention mechanism.pdf}
    \caption{Scaled dot-product self attention. The input token (voxel)
    embeddings $X$ are linearly projected onto queries $Q$, keys $K$, and
    values $V$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:qkv}). The similarity $QK^\top$ between every
    pair of positions is rescaled by $\sqrt{d_k}$ and normalized row-wise
    with a softmax to give the attention weights $A$, each row of which
    sums to one (Eq.~\ref{eq:attention_weights}). The output $Z$ is
    obtained by using $A$ to take a weighted sum of the value vectors for
    every position (Eq.~\ref{eq:attention_output}).}
    \label{fig:attention}
\end{figure}

This comes at a computational cost that grows quadratically with the
number of voxels, since forming $QK^\top$ requires comparing every
position with every other one: for $n$ voxels the attention matrix $A$ has
$n^2$ entries, so doubling the resolution of a 3D field increases the cost
of a single attention layer by a factor of $64$. This is why, throughout
this thesis, self attention is used only at the coarsest resolution inside
a U-Net's bottleneck, where there are relatively few voxels left to
compare, rather than at full field resolution.

\subsection{Normalizing flows}
\label{sec:normalizing_flows}

Several places in this thesis need a way to represent a complicated, high dimensional probability distribution , the posterior over cosmological parameters given some data, for instance , in a form that is both flexible enough to capture non Gaussian structure and simple enough to sample from and evaluate a likelihood for. A \emph{normalizing flow} is one way to build exactly such an object: start from a simple, tractable base distribution $p_0(\bm{z})$ (typically a standard multivariate Gaussian), and pass a sample $\bm{z}\sim p_0$ through an invertible, differentiable transformation $f_{\bm\theta}$ to obtain a sample $\bm{x} = f_{\bm\theta}(\bm{z})$ from the target distribution. Because $f_{\bm\theta}$ is invertible, the change-of variables formula gives an exact expression for the density of $\bm{x}$,
\begin{equation}
    p_{\bm\theta}(\bm{x}) = p_0\!\left(f_{\bm\theta}^{-1}(\bm{x})\right)\left|\det \frac{\partial f_{\bm\theta}^{-1}}{\partial \bm{x}}\right|,
\end{equation}
in terms of the (tractable) base density and the Jacobian determinant of the transformation. A single transformation of this kind is rarely expressive enough on its own, so in practice several are chained together, $\bm{x} = f_K\circ\cdots\circ f_1(\bm{z})$, each one designed (e.g.\ as an autoregressive or coupling-layer transform) so that its Jacobian determinant remains cheap to compute despite the added flexibility. Training then amounts to maximizing the log likelihood of the resulting density on a set of samples $\{\bm{x}_i\}$ directly, without needing a separate discriminator or a variational bound. A \emph{Masked Autoregressive Flow} (MAF)~\citep{papamakarios_masked_2018}, used in Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator} to model a posterior over cosmological parameters, is one widely used instance of this construction, in which each transform is autoregressive: every output dimension is a function of the base variable at that dimension and of all previously generated dimensions, which keeps the Jacobian triangular and hence trivial to invert and to compute a determinant for.

% [OPTION B: relocated from sec:lss_param_inference, with a new lead-in clause]
Beyond density estimation for parameter inference, normalizing flows have also been used to directly model the field level probability density of cosmological fields themselves: \citet{rouhiainen2021normalizingflowsrandomfields} learns an explicit, tractable representation of the field level probability density, conditional on cosmological parameters, from which both parameter posteriors and new field realizations can be sampled, while a wavelet-based multiscale flow similarly models the full field level likelihood of weak-lensing maps and can, as a byproduct, flag distribution shifts caused by unmodeled effects such as baryonic feedback~\citep{dai_multiscale_2024}.

\subsection{Simulation-based inference and neural posterior estimation}
\label{sec:sbi_npe}

Section~\ref{sec:lss_param_inference} already introduced the basic idea of simulation-based inference (SBI): rather than writing down an explicit likelihood $p(\bm{d}|\theta)$, generate a large number of simulated (parameter, data) pairs $\{(\theta_i, \bm{d}_i)\}$ and learn the relationship between the two directly. \emph{Neural posterior estimation} (NPE) is the specific version of this idea used in Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator}: a normalizing flow $q_{\bm\phi}(\theta\,|\,\bm{d})$ (Section~\ref{sec:normalizing_flows}), conditioned on the data $\bm{d}$, is trained to directly approximate the posterior $p(\theta|\bm{d})$, by maximizing the log probability the flow assigns to the true parameters $\theta_i$ given the corresponding simulated data $\bm{d}_i$, averaged over the simulated training set,
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\bm\phi} \ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log q_{\bm\phi}(\theta_i\,|\,\bm{d}_i).
\end{equation}
Because the flow is conditioned on $\bm{d}$ rather than trained for one fixed dataset, the same trained network can be evaluated on any new data $\bm{d}$ (simulated or, in principle, real) to obtain an approximate posterior $q_{\bm\phi}(\theta|\bm{d})\approx p(\theta|\bm{d})$ in a single forward pass, with no additional sampling or optimization required at inference time , unlike traditional likelihood-based approaches, which typically need to re-run an MCMC (or similar) sampler for every new dataset. This amortized cost is what makes NPE practical for the kind of large validation and test sets (hundreds of independent realizations) used in Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator}, where evaluating the posterior for each one individually via classical sampling would be far more expensive.

% [OPTION B: relocated from sec:lss_param_inference, with a new lead-in clause]
NPE is only one of several simulation-efficient strategies for building the neural density estimator itself, and a substantial methodological literature exists comparing them in a cosmological context. Sequential neural posterior estimation trains a conditional density estimator (typically a normalizing flow) directly on the posterior, adaptively concentrating simulations in the region of parameter space near the observed data rather than sampling the full prior uniformly~\citep[e.g.][]{greenberg2019automaticposteriortransformationlikelihoodfree}; neural ratio estimation instead trains a classifier to distinguish jointly from independently sampled parameter-data pairs, which in its truncated, marginal form estimates only the low dimensional marginal posteriors actually needed and has been shown to scale efficiently to the many nuisance parameter regime typical of CMB and other cosmological analyses, with a simulation budget that is close to independent of the number of nuisance parameters marginalized over~\citep{TMNRE,Cole_2022}. This idea of Truncated marginal neural ratio estimation has also been used in supernova cosmology and parameter inference~\citet{Karchev_2022}. Software packages such as \texttt{pydelfi}~\citep{Alsing_2019} combine this kind of density estimation with active learning, adaptively proposing new simulations where the current posterior estimate is least certain, and have demonstrated full posterior inference on real cosmological data (Type Ia supernovae, tomographic cosmic shear) with as few as $\mathcal{O}(10^3\text{,}10^4)$ simulations.

A complementary strategy, often combined with the above rather than competing with it, is to first \emph{compress} the data down to a small number of informative summary statistics before any density estimation is attempted, reducing the dimensionality that the density estimator itself has to handle. \emph{Information Maximizing Neural Networks} (IMNNs)~\citep{Charnock_2018,Makinen_2022} train a network to find the non linear summary of the data that maximizes the retained Fisher information, requiring only simulations generated around a single fiducial parameter value rather than across the full prior range.

\subsection{An introduction to diffusion models}
\label{sec:diffusion_intro}
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{chapters//ch0_introduction/diffusion.pdf}
    \caption{A visual depiction of a denoising diffusion model. The top arrow pointing right shows the forward corruption process, while the bottom arrow shows the generative process, with the $\bm{\mu_\theta}$ in red being the mean of the generative distribution, to be learned and predicted by a denoising network.}
    \label{fig:diffusion_process}
\end{figure}

Diffusion models~\citep{sohl-dickstein_deep_2015,ho_denoising_2020} are a class of generative model built around a simple idea: it is easy to destroy structure in data by gradually adding noise, and if a neural network can learn to reverse this process step by step, it can turn pure noise back into a realistic sample. Concretely, given a data sample $\bm{x}_0$ (in our case, a cosmological field such as a dark matter density map), the \emph{forward process} (Figure~\ref{fig:diffusion_process}) defines a sequence of increasingly noisy versions $\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2,\dots,\bm{x}_T$ by repeatedly mixing in a small amount of Gaussian noise,
\begin{equation}
    q(\bm{x}_t\,|\,\bm{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\bm{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_t\,\bm{I}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ is a (small, pre specified) noise-schedule parameter at step $t$, and $\mathcal{N}(\bm{x};\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})$ denotes a Gaussian distribution with mean $\bm{\mu}$ and covariance $\bm{\Sigma}$. Because the composition of Gaussians is itself Gaussian, this process has a convenient closed form that lets us jump directly from the clean data $\bm{x}_0$ to any noise level $t$ without simulating every intermediate step,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward_closed_form}
    q(\bm{x}_t\,|\,\bm{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{x}_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\bm{I}\right), \qquad \bar\alpha_t \equiv \prod_{s=1}^t (1-\beta_s).
\end{equation}
For a sufficiently large number of steps $T$ and an appropriately chosen schedule $\{\beta_t\}$, $\bar\alpha_T\approx0$, and $\bm{x}_T$ is (to excellent approximation) indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise, regardless of the original field $\bm{x}_0$ , all of the original cosmological information has been destroyed.

The generative model is obtained by learning to \emph{reverse} this process: starting from pure noise $\bm{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})$, a neural network is trained to gradually remove noise, step by step, until a clean sample $\bm{x}_0$ emerges. Each reverse step is itself modelled as a Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
    p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t-1}\,|\,\bm{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}_{t-1};\ \bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,t),\ \sigma_t^2\bm{I}\right),
\end{equation}
where the mean $\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}$ is predicted by a neural network with parameters $\bm{\theta}$ (in practice, a U-Net , see Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi} , that takes the noisy field $\bm{x}_t$ and the current noise level $t$ as input). \footnote{In practice, $\sigma_t^2$ is often set to the forward process variance or left as a learnable parameter. For our work, we use $\sigma_t^2$ is learned during training(see Section~\ref{sec:vdm_elbo}).} Rather than predicting $\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}$ directly, it is both simpler and empirically more effective to instead train the network to predict the \emph{noise} $\bm{\epsilon}$ that was added to produce $\bm{x}_t$ from $\bm{x}_0$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:forward_closed_form}, i.e.\ a network $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,t)$ such that $\bm{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{\epsilon}$, with $\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})$. This turns training into a straightforward regression problem: sample a training field $\bm{x}_0$, sample a random timestep $t$ and noise $\bm{\epsilon}$, form the corresponding noisy field $\bm{x}_t$, and minimize the simple loss
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffusion_loss}
    \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\bm{x}_0,\,\bm{\epsilon}}\left[\ \left\lVert\, \bm{\epsilon} - \bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{\epsilon},\ t\right)\right\rVert^2\ \right].
\end{equation}
This loss, while simple to state, can be derived rigorously as a (simplified, reweighted) variational lower bound on the data log likelihood~\citep{sohl-dickstein_deep_2015,ho_denoising_2020,kingma_variational_2023} , i.e.\ diffusion models are, under the hood, latent-variable models trained by maximizing an evidence lower bound (ELBO), with $\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_T$ playing the role of a sequence of latent variables.

The ``evidence lower bound'' name is worth unpacking, since it is really just a statement about a specific measure of distance between two probability distributions, the \emph{Kullback,Leibler (KL) divergence},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl_div}
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q) = \int p(\bm{x})\,\log\frac{p(\bm{x})}{q(\bm{x})}\,\text{d}\bm{x} \ \geq 0,
\end{equation}
which vanishes if and only if $p=q$ everywhere, but is not symmetric in $p$ and $q$ in general. In a latent-variable model such as this one, the log likelihood of the data can be written exactly as the ELBO plus a KL-divergence term that is always non negative; maximizing the ELBO is therefore equivalent to maximizing a guaranteed lower bound on the true log likelihood, with the (intractable) gap between the two set by exactly this KL divergence between the model's approximate posterior over the latents and the true one. Concretely for a diffusion model, the ELBO itself decomposes into a sum of KL divergences between the (Gaussian) forward and reverse distributions at each timestep $t$, which is what the simplified $\epsilon$-prediction loss of Eq.~\ref{eq:diffusion_loss} is, up to a $t$-dependent reweighting, actually minimizing.

An equivalent and highly influential perspective~\citep{song_score-based_2021} recasts the entire forward/reverse process as a stochastic differential equation (SDE), in which the trained network $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}$ is directly related to the \emph{score} of the noised data distribution, $\nabla_{\bm{x}_t}\log q(\bm{x}_t)$; this score-based SDE viewpoint unifies diffusion models with earlier score-matching approaches and provides additional flexibility in the choice of noise schedule and sampler.

Once trained, new samples are generated by starting from Gaussian noise $\bm{x}_T$ and iteratively applying the learned reverse step for $t=T,T-1,\dots,1$, at each step using the network's noise prediction $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,t)$ to estimate (and subtract off) the noise, recovering progressively cleaner fields until $\bm{x}_0$ is reached. Conditional generation , central to this thesis, e.g.\ generating a 21\,cm brightness-temperature field conditional on a dark matter field, or a high resolution field conditional on a low resolution one , is achieved by additionally passing the conditioning field into the network at every step, $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,\,t,\,\bm{x}_\text{cond})$, so that the reverse process learns to denoise \emph{towards} samples consistent with the given condition rather than towards the unconditional data distribution.

Diffusion models have rapidly become the dominant approach for high fidelity image and video generation~\citep{dhariwal_diffusion_2021}, owing to their training stability (compared to, e.g., generative adversarial networks) and their ability to produce diverse, high quality samples. Their extension to volumetric (3D) scientific data, and specifically to cosmological fields, is comparatively recent and is the subject of Chapters~\ref{ch:lodi} and~\ref{ch:cosmofold} of this thesis.

\subsection{Diffusion posterior sampling}
\label{sec:dps_intro}

Everything above describes \emph{generating} a field, either unconditionally or conditioned on another field known exactly. A related but distinct problem, addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:dps} and used in Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos}, is \emph{inverse} problems: given a corrupted, incomplete, or otherwise indirect observation $\bm{y}$ of an underlying field $\bm{x}_0$ , related to it through some known (or approximately known) forward process, e.g.\ noise, masking, or a survey selection function , how does one recover the posterior distribution $p(\bm{x}_0|\bm{y})$ over the field that could have produced it? \emph{Diffusion posterior sampling} (DPS)~\citep{chung_diffusion_2023} answers this without training a new, problem-specific network for every choice of corruption: instead, a single \emph{unconditionally} pretrained diffusion prior (of exactly the kind described above) is reused, and at each step of the reverse process an additional guidance term, derived from how well the current estimate of $\bm{x}_0$ is consistent with the observation $\bm{y}$, nudges the sample towards the corrected posterior rather than the unconditional prior. This turns a single pretrained generative model into a flexible Bayesian prior that can be reused across many different inverse problems , exactly the property exploited in Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos} to fold in an external cosmological prior, and in Chapter~\ref{ch:dps} to reconstruct dark matter and Lyman-$\alpha$ fields from corrupted or indirect observations. The full derivation of the guidance term, and its practical implementation, is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:dps}.

In the specific context of 21 cm IM theoretical modeling, several recent efforts aim at predicting the brightness temperature signal at the field level, including fast generative models of HI maps using normalizing flows~\citep{hassan_hiflow_2022}, the neural approach of \citet{wadekar_hinet_2021}, and a generative adversarial framework introduced by \citet{andrianomena_emulating_2022}. Diffusion models have thus far found very limited application in this domain~\citep{ono_debiasing_2024}, and none specifically within 21 cm IM. To address this, we develop a novel, physically motivated ML pipeline (\LODI) which, in a first step, uses a U-Net to learn the mapping between dark matter and halos, and in a second step employs a custom-designed diffusion model to predict the 21 cm signal down to very small, non linear scales. Predicting both halos and the 21 cm signal jointly is crucial for using such machinery in cross-correlation studies of the IM signal with other LSS tracers (e.g.\ lensing, galaxies), which is the ultimate aim of this line of work. This should be viewed as the first demonstration of a diffusion-based, halo-conditioned approach to 21 cm IM, complementary to existing forward modeling methods.

Despite these advances, a major challenge remains in scaling generative models to arbitrarily large, high fidelity cosmological volumes, especially when constrained by GPU memory and computational cost. Moreover, the evolution of cosmic structure is inherently non local: long-range gravitational interactions couple distant regions, and baryonic feedback processes , such as those driven by supernovae or active galactic nuclei , can redistribute matter over large scales. Accurately modeling these effects requires a network to learn global dependencies rather than simple local conditional relationships, which becomes particularly challenging when no similar low resolution reference field is available to guide the generation.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce \FOLD (``Cosmological Field Overlap Latent Diffusion''), a technique building on the variational diffusion model framework \LODI presented above and in~\cite{mishra2025largefastaccuratehi}. \FOLD enables the rapid and accurate generation of large three dimensional cosmological fields by conditioning the denoising process on the underlying large scale matter distribution, represented by either the stellar density or dark matter density fields. We demonstrate the capability of this approach by generating gas temperature fields conditioned on dark matter density, as well as dark matter density fields reconstructed from sparse stellar density distributions, using the IllustrisTNG TNG300 simulations. These two applications are particularly challenging and relevant since both are intrinsically sensitive to small scale physical processes (star formation and galactic feedback) that also affect intermediate cosmological scales. Their importance is also reflected in summary statistics such as the power spectrum: it is well known that baryonic processes, as simulated in state of the art hydrodynamical simulations, produce a suppression of power at $k \sim 1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ caused by feedback, and a steep increase of power at smaller scales $k \gg 1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ from stars forming in the innermost parts of dark matter haloes~\citep{chisari19,schneider15,vandaalen20}. While the exact amount of suppression and increase of power varies among different simulation suites, the shape of these effects is a common prediction across most models , making the temperature and stellar fields a natural choice for testing our method's performance across both regimes.

\FOLD is trained on just $\approx 1\%$ and validated on $\approx 0.5\%$ of the simulation volume, before being applied to synthesize complete large scale realizations, with excellent performance across 1-, 2-, and 3-point statistics. It naturally enforces periodic boundary conditions and surpasses previous overlapping patch based approaches by eliminating edge artifacts and maintaining structural coherence, even when conditioned on highly sparse stellar input.

A related, complementary goal , currently hampered by the same computational bottlenecks , is to complement traditional summary statistics (such as two  and three point statistics) with more informative, data driven summaries that better capture non Gaussian features of evolved non linear fields (e.g.~\citep{bairagi2025patchnethierarchicalapproachneural,Borg,lanzieri2025optimalneuralsummarisationfullfield,Bayer_2025}).

\section{Thesis Outline}

This thesis presents a set of complementary contributions to field level, diffusion-based and emulation-based modeling of cosmological and astrophysical fields, built around the same idea running through this Chapter: extracting the cosmological information that gets thrown away when a field is compressed into a summary statistic, using generative models trained directly on simulations.

Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi} (\LODI) describes the halo-conditioned diffusion pipeline for predicting the 21\,cm signal: a U-Net first maps dark matter to halos, then a custom diffusion model paints the 21\,cm brightness temperature field on top, down to small non linear scales, with a latent-overlap scheme to scale the generation up to volumes larger than the network was trained on.

Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmofold} (\FOLD) extends the same diffusion framework to large volume field generation via field overlap latent diffusion, generating gas temperature fields from dark matter and dark matter fields from sparse stellar density, on the IllustrisTNG TNG300 volume, together with a positional-encoding scheme aimed at improving the recovered bispectrum.

Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos} extends the \FOLD architecture to field level \emph{posterior} sampling of the dark matter field conditional on a tracer, and shows how an external cosmological prior (from Planck) can be incorporated into the generative sampling procedure via Bayes' theorem, without retraining the network.

Chapter~\ref{ch:dps} develops the general framework of diffusion posterior sampling for inverse problems that Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos} builds on, and applies it to two further problems: reconstructing the dark matter field from noisy or incomplete data, and reconstructing the baryonic and dark matter quantities along a line of sight from an observed Lyman-$\alpha$ forest spectrum.

Chapter~\ref{ch:emulator} moves from a single tracer to a multi-tracer setting, using a conditional U-Net emulator to generate galaxy and HI fields from dark matter, and combining them in a simulation-based inference pipeline to show what is gained , in terms of constraining power on $\Om$ and $\sige$ , by moving from power-spectrum-based inference to full 3D fieldlevel inference, and from a single tracer to two combined.

We conclude in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} with a discussion of the common thread running through these chapters and promising avenues for future work, including applications to cross-correlation studies between 21\,cm IM and other LSS tracers, and to more realistic, survey like data.
