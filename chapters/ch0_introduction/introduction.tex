\chapter{Introduction}

\section{The Cosmological Context}

Neutral (atomic) hydrogen (HI) plays an important role in cosmology and structure formation processes: its distribution follows the underlying matter density field, and for most of the cosmic history it constitutes the reservoir of baryons to fuel star formation. This makes it a powerful and novel tracer of the large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe~\citep[e.g.][]{review,Ansari_2012,Santos:2015,villa14}. While Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) observations~\citep{WMAP,planck:2018,ACT} and galaxy redshift surveys~\citep[e.g.][]{BOSS:2016} have significantly constrained the parameters of the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model, key questions are still unresolved. In particular, the fundamental nature of dark matter and dark energy is still unknown, and persistent tensions between different cosmological measurements have yet to be fully understood, including the recent tentative evidence for evolving dark energy~\citep[e.g.][]{riess:2019,Verde:2019,wong:2019,DESIde}. Mapping the large-scale distribution of HI and tracking its evolution over cosmic time offer a complementary approach to traditional galaxy surveys, by probing large volumes at high redshifts, and thus potentially providing stress tests for many cosmological models in new regimes~\citep[e.g.][]{bull:2015,villa15,obuljen_high-redshift_2018,berti_constraining_2022,Villaescusa-Navarro:2016}.

The 21 cm line, which results from the spin-flip transition within the hyperfine structure of the ground state of neutral hydrogen (see~\citet{Furlanetto:2006}), is redshifted by the cosmological expansion and can be observed at radio wavelengths. Many experiments, including interferometric arrays like CHIME~\citep{Bandura:2014gwa,CHIMEdetection}, CHORD, and HIRAX~\citep{Newburgh:2016mwi}, and single-dish instruments such as GBT~\citep{Masui2013,Wolz2022} and FAST~\citep{Hu:2019okh}, aim to detect this signal using intensity mapping (IM) techniques~\citep{Bharadwaj:2000,Battye:2004,McQuinn:2005,Chang:2007,Seo:2009fq,Battye:2013,Kovetz:2017}. Several of these efforts have already achieved detections through cross-correlation with optical galaxy surveys~\citep{Chang2010,Masui2013,Anderson2018,Wolz2022,Cunnington:2022,Paul2023}.

Radio cosmology is also a key science objective for the SKA Observatory (SKAO),\footnote{\url{https://www.skao.int/}} which will comprise two major arrays: SKA-Low in Australia and SKA-Mid in South Africa. In particular, SKA-Mid, when operated in single-dish mode~\citep[e.g.][]{Santos:2015,Bacon:2018}, will be capable of conducting 21 cm IM surveys across cosmologically relevant scales out to redshift $z \sim 3$. Currently under construction, the SKAO has a working precursor, MeerKAT, which is already contributing through its cosmological IM survey, MeerKLASS~\citep{Santos:2017}. Early results from MeerKAT data have been encouraging~\citep{Wang:2021,irfan2022}, including a first cross-correlation detection with WiggleZ galaxy data~\citep{Cunnington:2022uzo}.

Alongside several technical efforts mainly focused on foreground modeling and mitigation, refining the forecast capabilities of 21 cm IM, both as a standalone probe and in combination with other cosmological observables, is crucial (e.g.~\citep{carucci_cross-correlation_2017,berti_21cm_2023,berti24}). These forecasts are essential not only for strengthening the scientific case for 21 cm IM radio cosmology, but also in order to optimize the design and strategy of upcoming surveys.

The large-scale structure (LSS) traced by these observables is itself the end product of a well-understood but analytically intractable evolutionary history. According to the standard inflationary paradigm, the initial density perturbations that seeded structure formation were, to very high precision, a Gaussian random field, fully characterized by its two-point statistics (the power spectrum) and its early-time transfer function~\citep[e.g.][]{planck:2018}. Under gravitational instability, these perturbations grow, and while linear perturbation theory accurately describes their evolution on large scales and at early times, the growth becomes non-linear once density contrasts approach unity, first on small scales and progressively on larger ones as cosmic time advances. The result is the LSS observed today: a highly non-Gaussian density field, organized into the filamentary cosmic web of halos, voids, and filaments~\citep[e.g.][]{obuljen23,villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}.

This non-Gaussianity has direct consequences for how much cosmological information can be extracted from LSS observables. For a Gaussian field, the power spectrum is a complete statistical description, and all cosmological information is contained within it. Once gravitational (and, for baryonic tracers, astrophysical) non-linearities become important, however, this is no longer true: information leaks into higher-order correlations (the bispectrum, trispectrum, and beyond), and standard perturbative techniques used to model these statistics break down at the mildly non-linear and non-linear scales that are often the most information-rich~\citep[e.g.][]{obuljen23}. Restricting analyses to the two-point function alone therefore discards a substantial fraction of the cosmological information encoded in the field, particularly on the small scales probed by upcoming high-resolution 21 cm IM and galaxy surveys.

This has motivated a shift, over the last several years, toward \emph{field-level} inference and emulation: rather than compressing a cosmological field into a small set of hand-chosen summary statistics before performing inference, one instead works directly with the field itself (or a learned representation of it), in principle retaining the full non-Gaussian information content~\citep[e.g.][]{jamieson_field-level_2023,angulo_large-scale_2022}. A representative example of this direction is the work of \citet{rouhiainen_cosmology_2024}, which explicitly frames the non-Gaussianity of the late-time, small-scale LSS as the central obstacle to analytic modeling, and addresses it by learning the field-level probability density of the matter distribution directly with normalizing flows    explicitly analogous to how gravitational evolution transforms an initially Gaussian field into today's complex non-Gaussian one    before turning to denoising diffusion models for volumetric, 3D super-resolution tasks. The approach taken in this thesis shares this field-level philosophy: rather than relying on summary statistics or hand-calibrated halo-occupation-style prescriptions, we aim to learn the field-level mapping between cosmological tracers directly from simulations, retaining small-scale non-Gaussian structure that summary-statistic-based or purely perturbative approaches would otherwise discard.

\subsection{Growth of structure}

To make the qualitative picture above quantitative, it is useful to work directly with the matter overdensity field,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(\bm{r},t)\equiv\frac{\rho(\bm{r},t)-\langle\rho(\bm{r},t)\rangle}{\langle\rho(\bm{r},t)\rangle},
\end{equation}
where $\langle...\rangle$ denotes an ensemble average. On sub-horizon scales and at times well after recombination, $\delta$ obeys, in Fourier space,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(a^2(t)\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\delta(\bm{k},t)\right)=4\pi G a^2(t)\,\rho(t)\,\delta(\bm{k},t),
\end{equation}
sourced by the Poisson equation $k^2\Phi = \tfrac{3}{2}\Om \hub^2\,\delta(\bm{k},a)/a$. It is standard to factorize the time and scale dependence of the linear solution by introducing the growth factor $D_+(a)$ and transfer function $T(k)$,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(\bm{k},a) = \frac{2k^2}{5\Om \hub^2}\,\mathcal{R}(\bm{k})\,T(k)\,D_+(a),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}(\bm{k})$ encodes the primordial (inflationary) fluctuations. The growth factor satisfies $D_+(a)\propto a$ (matter domination) at early times, and is suppressed at late times by the cosmological constant, decaying relative to $a$ as dark-energy domination sets in    explicitly,
\begin{equation}
    D_+(a) = a \; {}_2F_1\!\left(\frac{1}{3},\,1;\,\frac{11}{6};\,-\frac{\Omega_\Lambda}{\Om}a^3\right),
\end{equation}
in terms of the hypergeometric function ${}_2F_1$~\citep[e.g.][]{Weinberg2008}.

The (linear) matter power spectrum, defined through
\begin{equation}
    (2\pi)^3 \Pk\,\delta_\text{D}^3(\bm{k}+\bm{k}') = \langle\delta(\bm{k})\delta(\bm{k}')\rangle,
\end{equation}
inherits this factorization as $P_\text{Lin}(k,a) \propto T^2(k)\,D_+^2(a)$. Crucially, \emph{for a Gaussian field, the power spectrum is a complete statistical description}: every higher-order correlator $\langle\delta(\bm{k}_1)\cdots\delta(\bm{k}_n)\rangle$ either vanishes (odd $n$) or reduces to a sum of products of two-point functions (even $n$), a consequence of Wick's theorem. The amplitude of this power spectrum is conventionally summarized by $\sige$, the RMS overdensity in spheres of radius $8\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}$,
\begin{equation}
    \sige^2(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty \text{d}k\; k^2\, P_\text{Lin}(k,a)\, W^2(k R),\qquad R = 8\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc},
\end{equation}
with $W$ a top-hat filter in Fourier space~\citep[e.g.][]{Planck:2018parameters}.

\subsection{Nonlinear large-scale structure}

The linear description above breaks down once fluctuations become of order unity. This defines a nonlinear scale $k_\text{NL}$ through the dimensionless power spectrum,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^2(k_\text{NL}) \equiv \frac{k_\text{NL}^3}{2\pi^2}\,P_\text{Lin}(k_\text{NL}) \sim 1,
\end{equation}
which for present-day $\Lambda$CDM parameters occurs at $k_\text{NL}\sim0.2\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$~\citep[e.g.][]{Takahashi_2012}    squarely within the regime our field-level ML models are designed to capture. Beyond this scale, non-Gaussianity manifests directly as non-vanishing higher-order statistics, most simply the bispectrum,
\begin{equation}
    (2\pi)^3 B(k_1,k_2,k_3)\,\delta_\text{D}^3(\bm{k}_1+\bm{k}_2+\bm{k}_3) = \langle\delta(\bm{k}_1)\delta(\bm{k}_2)\delta(\bm{k}_3)\rangle,
\end{equation}
which vanishes identically for a Gaussian field but carries additional, independent cosmological information once gravitational (and, for baryonic tracers, astrophysical feedback) non-linearities are significant~\citep[e.g.][]{Sefusatti_2006}. Standard cosmological perturbation theory can, in principle, extend the analytic description into this regime order-by-order (e.g.\ at ``1-loop'' order), but the required loop integrals grow rapidly in complexity, and even at next-to-leading order the perturbative bispectrum already departs from full $N$-body results by an order of magnitude on the mildly non-linear scales relevant to this work~\citep[e.g.][]{Chudaykin_2020}. This breakdown of analytic and perturbative methods precisely on the scales that carry the most cosmological information is the central motivation, throughout this thesis, for turning to machine-learning-based, field-level approaches instead.

\subsection{Large-scale structure for cosmological parameter inference}
\label{sec:lss_param_inference}

The physics above directly shapes how the LSS is used in practice to constrain cosmological parameters such as $\Om$ and $\sige$. The traditional approach compresses an observed field (a galaxy catalogue, a weak-lensing map, an intensity map) into a small set of hand-chosen summary statistics    most commonly the power spectrum, and increasingly the bispectrum    and performs likelihood-based (or Gaussian-likelihood) inference on the resulting data vector, using perturbation theory or fitting functions such as HaloFit~\citep{Takahashi_2012} to model the observable as a function of $\bm{\phi}=(\Om,\sige,\dots)$. This approach is well understood and computationally cheap, but, as argued above, it is information-lossy by construction on the mildly non-linear and non-linear scales where perturbation theory itself begins to fail~\citep[e.g.][]{Chudaykin_2020}.

Two developments over roughly the last decade have pushed beyond this. First, \emph{simulation-based inference} (SBI, also called likelihood-free inference) sidesteps the need for an analytic likelihood altogether: cosmological simulations are run over a grid or Latin hypercube of parameters $\bm{\phi}$, and a learned surrogate (e.g.\ a neural density estimator) is trained on the resulting $(\bm{x},\bm{\phi})$ pairs to approximate the posterior $p(\bm{\phi}|\bm{x})$ directly~\citep[e.g.][]{jeffrey_likelihood-free_2020,angulo_large-scale_2022}. Large public simulation suites such as CAMELS~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_camels} were built explicitly to enable this kind of approach across a wide range of both cosmological and astrophysical (feedback) parameters. Second, and building on SBI, \emph{field-level inference} removes the summary-statistic compression step entirely, training models to infer $\bm{\phi}$ (or a full field-level posterior) directly from the observed field itself, in principle saturating the available cosmological information rather than only what survives compression into a power spectrum or bispectrum~\citep[e.g.][]{jamieson_field-level_2023}. Normalizing-flow-based approaches, such as that of \citet{rouhiainen_cosmology_2024}, represent current state-of-the-art examples of this field-level philosophy, learning an explicit, tractable representation of the field-level probability density    conditional on cosmological parameters    from which both parameter posteriors and new field realizations can be sampled.

This thesis adopts the same field-level philosophy, but applied to a different pair of problems: rather than parameter inference from an observed field, we are concerned with learning the field-level \emph{mapping between cosmological tracers} (dark matter, halos, 21\,cm brightness temperature, baryonic fields) directly from simulations. The two goals are complementary, however: an accurate field-level emulator of the kind developed in this thesis is itself a natural forward-simulator for the SBI/field-level parameter-inference pipelines described above, since it can generate the large number of realistic, high-resolution training pairs that such methods require far more cheaply than full hydrodynamical simulations.

\section{The Simulation Bottleneck}

Cosmological simulations are essential tools for understanding the formation and evolution of structure in the Universe. They allow us to explore the complex interplay of gravitational, hydrodynamic, and astrophysical processes shaping the distribution of matter from large down to highly non-linear galactic and sub-galactic scales. However, high-fidelity hydrodynamical simulations are computationally very expensive, limiting their applicability when the parameter space is large in inference studies. For example, the IllustrisTNG simulations~\citep{nelson2021illustristngsimulationspublicdata,Pillepich_2017} required approximately 35 million CPU-hours for the largest \TNG run and about 18 million CPU-hours for TNG100, while EAGLE~\citep{EAGLE} consumed roughly 4.5 million CPU-hours.

Such computational demands become particularly prohibitive for cosmological or astrophysical field-level inference and simulation-based inference (SBI) frameworks, which require large ensembles of conditional forward simulations to explore the parameter spaces. Recent works have shown promising applications, including the cosmological context~\citep{angulo_large-scale_2022,jeffrey_likelihood-free_2020,bairagi2025simulationsneedsimulationbasedinference,Zeghal_2025,karchev2023simsimssimulationbasedsupernovaia,Saxena_2024,tucci24}. However, their applicability remains limited by the requirement of generating sufficient high-fidelity simulations. This computational bottleneck underlines the need for fast, accurate surrogate models capable of reproducing cosmological fields at a fraction of the cost — not limited to dark matter, but crucially including baryonic observables that can be directly constrained with data.

This same bottleneck also constrains theoretical modeling of the 21 cm signal itself, which currently follows three main approaches: halo models (see~\citet{padmanabhan_hi_2023}), perturbation theory (e.g.~\citep{obuljen23}), or hydrodynamical simulations of structure formation incorporating the relevant physical processes (e.g.~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}). Each of these methods has both advantages and disadvantages. For example, modeling the IM signal requires both large volumes and high resolution to fully resolve the physics of the small mass ($\sim$ 10$^{10}$ M$_{\odot}/h$) dark matter halos that host HI, something that is difficult to achieve with full hydrodynamical simulations due to the range of scales involved. In the context of halo models, extensive work has been expended to study the mass limit for halos to retain HI, for example by looking at the damped Lyman-$\alpha$ systems statistics, both from the point of view of halo models~\citep{Padmanabhan_2016} and observationally~\citep{Dev_2023,Obuljen_2019}. In addition to these semi-analytical and perturbative frameworks, several forward-modeling approaches have been developed to predict the HI field directly from dark-matter simulations by prescribing or calibrating the $M_{\rm HI}(M)$ relation and other parameters on observations or hydrodynamical runs~\citep[e.g.][]{hitz2025fastsimulationcosmologicalneutral,li2022theoreticalmodelsatomichydrogen,spinelli_atomic_2020}. These methods, however, extend predictions to large volumes by applying calibrated prescriptions for the HI  halo connection, rather than learning the full field-level mapping from simulations. Thus, a method capable of learning the small-scale physics from state-of-the-art simulations while at the same time reaching large scales and volumes would be of great importance — this is the computational and modeling gap this thesis addresses.


\section{Machine Learning for Field-Level Cosmology}

In recent years, machine learning techniques, and in particular generative models, have emerged as powerful emulators to reproduce cosmological and astrophysical fields~\citep{perraudin2019cosmologicalnbodysimulationschallenge,Li_2021,jamieson_field-level_2023,Rigo_2025,hassan_hiflow_2022,andrianomena_emulating_2022,sharma25,bernardini2025ember2,andrianomena2024cosmologicalmultifieldemulator}. Among them, \emph{diffusion models} have emerged as the current state of the art for high-fidelity generative modeling, and are the central tool used throughout this thesis. Given their importance to everything that follows, we introduce them here in some detail.

\subsection{An introduction to diffusion models}
\label{sec:diffusion_intro}

Diffusion models~\citep{sohl-dickstein_deep_2015,ho_denoising_2020} are a class of generative model built around a simple idea: it is easy to destroy structure in data by gradually adding noise, and if a neural network can learn to reverse this process step by step, it can turn pure noise back into a realistic sample. Concretely, given a data sample $\bm{x}_0$ (in our case, a cosmological field such as a dark matter density map), the \emph{forward process} defines a sequence of increasingly noisy versions $\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2,\dots,\bm{x}_T$ by repeatedly mixing in a small amount of Gaussian noise,
\begin{equation}
    q(\bm{x}_t\,|\,\bm{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\bm{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_t\,\bm{I}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ is a (small, pre-specified) noise-schedule parameter at step $t$, and $\mathcal{N}(\bm{x};\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})$ denotes a Gaussian distribution with mean $\bm{\mu}$ and covariance $\bm{\Sigma}$. Because the composition of Gaussians is itself Gaussian, this process has a convenient closed form that lets us jump directly from the clean data $\bm{x}_0$ to any noise level $t$ without simulating every intermediate step,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward_closed_form}
    q(\bm{x}_t\,|\,\bm{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{x}_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\bm{I}\right), \qquad \bar\alpha_t \equiv \prod_{s=1}^t (1-\beta_s).
\end{equation}
For a sufficiently large number of steps $T$ and an appropriately chosen schedule $\{\beta_t\}$, $\bar\alpha_T\approx0$, and $\bm{x}_T$ is (to excellent approximation) indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise, regardless of the original field $\bm{x}_0$    all of the original cosmological information has been destroyed.

The generative model is obtained by learning to \emph{reverse} this process: starting from pure noise $\bm{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})$, a neural network is trained to gradually remove noise, step by step, until a clean sample $\bm{x}_0$ emerges. Each reverse step is itself modelled as a Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
    p_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{t-1}\,|\,\bm{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{x}_{t-1};\ \bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,t),\ \sigma_t^2\bm{I}\right),
\end{equation}
where the mean $\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}$ is predicted by a neural network with parameters $\bm{\theta}$ (in practice, a U-Net    see Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi}    that takes the noisy field $\bm{x}_t$ and the current noise level $t$ as input). Rather than predicting $\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\theta}}$ directly, it is both simpler and empirically more effective to instead train the network to predict the \emph{noise} $\bm{\epsilon}$ that was added to produce $\bm{x}_t$ from $\bm{x}_0$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:forward_closed_form}, i.e.\ a network $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,t)$ such that $\bm{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{\epsilon}$, with $\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})$. This turns training into a straightforward regression problem: sample a training field $\bm{x}_0$, sample a random timestep $t$ and noise $\bm{\epsilon}$, form the corresponding noisy field $\bm{x}_t$, and minimize the simple loss
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffusion_loss}
    \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\bm{x}_0,\,\bm{\epsilon}}\left[\ \left\lVert\, \bm{\epsilon} - \bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\bm{\epsilon},\ t\right)\right\rVert^2\ \right].
\end{equation}
This loss, while simple to state, can be derived rigorously as a (simplified, reweighted) variational lower bound on the data log-likelihood~\citep{sohl-dickstein_deep_2015,ho_denoising_2020,kingma_variational_2023}    i.e.\ diffusion models are, under the hood, latent-variable models trained by maximizing an evidence lower bound (ELBO), with $\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_T$ playing the role of a sequence of latent variables. An equivalent and highly influential perspective~\citep{song_score-based_2021} recasts the entire forward/reverse process as a stochastic differential equation (SDE), in which the trained network $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}$ is directly related to the \emph{score} of the noised data distribution, $\nabla_{\bm{x}_t}\log q(\bm{x}_t)$; this score-based SDE viewpoint unifies diffusion models with earlier score-matching approaches and provides additional flexibility in the choice of noise schedule and sampler.

Once trained, new samples are generated by starting from Gaussian noise $\bm{x}_T$ and iteratively applying the learned reverse step for $t=T,T-1,\dots,1$, at each step using the network's noise prediction $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,t)$ to estimate (and subtract off) the noise, recovering progressively cleaner fields until $\bm{x}_0$ is reached. Conditional generation    central to this thesis, e.g.\ generating a 21\,cm brightness-temperature field conditional on a dark matter field, or a high-resolution field conditional on a low-resolution one    is achieved by additionally passing the conditioning field into the network at every step, $\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_t,\,t,\,\bm{x}_\text{cond})$, so that the reverse process learns to denoise \emph{towards} samples consistent with the given condition rather than towards the unconditional data distribution.

Diffusion models have rapidly become the dominant approach for high-fidelity image and video generation~\citep{dhariwal_diffusion_2021}, owing to their training stability (compared to, e.g., generative adversarial networks) and their ability to produce diverse, high-quality samples. Their extension to volumetric (3D) scientific data, and specifically to cosmological fields, is comparatively recent and is the subject of Chapters~\ref{ch:lodi} and~\ref{ch:cosmofold} of this thesis.
%   - END NEW 4                                                       

In the specific context of 21 cm IM theoretical modeling, several recent efforts aim at predicting the brightness temperature signal at the field level, including fast generative models of HI maps using normalizing flows~\citep{hassan_hiflow_2022}, the neural approach of \citet{wadekar_hinet_2021}, and a generative adversarial framework introduced by \citet{andrianomena_emulating_2022}. Diffusion models have thus far found very limited application in this domain~\citep{ono_debiasing_2024}, and none specifically within 21 cm IM. To address this, we develop a novel, physically motivated ML pipeline (\LODI) which, in a first step, uses a U-Net to learn the mapping between dark matter and halos, and in a second step employs a custom-designed diffusion model to predict the 21 cm signal down to very small, non-linear scales. Predicting both halos and the 21 cm signal jointly is crucial for using such machinery in cross-correlation studies of the IM signal with other LSS tracers (e.g.\ lensing, galaxies), which is the ultimate aim of this line of work. This should be viewed as the first demonstration of a diffusion-based, halo-conditioned approach to 21 cm IM, complementary to existing forward-modeling methods.

Despite these advances, a major challenge remains in scaling generative models to arbitrarily large, high-fidelity cosmological volumes, especially when constrained by GPU memory and computational cost. Moreover, the evolution of cosmic structure is inherently non-local: long-range gravitational interactions couple distant regions, and baryonic feedback processes — such as those driven by supernovae or active galactic nuclei — can redistribute matter over large scales. Accurately modeling these effects requires a network to learn global dependencies rather than simple local conditional relationships, which becomes particularly challenging when no similar low-resolution reference field is available to guide the generation.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce \FOLD (``Cosmological Field Overlap Latent Diffusion''), a technique building on the variational diffusion model framework \LODI presented above and in~\cite{mishra2025largefastaccuratehi}. \FOLD enables the rapid and accurate generation of large three-dimensional cosmological fields by conditioning the denoising process on the underlying large-scale matter distribution, represented by either the stellar density or dark matter density fields. We demonstrate the capability of this approach by generating gas temperature fields conditioned on dark matter density, as well as dark matter density fields reconstructed from sparse stellar density distributions, using the IllustrisTNG  TNG300 simulations. These two applications are particularly challenging and relevant since both are intrinsically sensitive to small-scale physical processes (star formation and galactic feedback) that also affect intermediate cosmological scales. Their importance is also reflected in summary statistics such as the power spectrum: it is well known that baryonic processes, as simulated in state-of-the-art hydrodynamical simulations, produce a suppression of power at $k \sim 1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ caused by feedback, and a steep increase of power at smaller scales $k \gg 1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ from stars forming in the innermost parts of dark matter haloes~\citep{chisari19,schneider15,vandaalen20}. While the exact amount of suppression and increase of power varies among different simulation suites, the shape of these effects is a common prediction across most models — making the temperature and stellar fields a natural choice for testing our method's performance across both regimes.

\FOLD is trained on just $\approx 1\%$ and validated on $\approx 0.5\%$ of the simulation volume, before being applied to synthesize complete large-scale realizations, with excellent performance across 1-, 2-, and 3-point statistics. It naturally enforces periodic boundary conditions and surpasses previous overlapping patch-based approaches by eliminating edge artifacts and maintaining structural coherence, even when conditioned on highly sparse stellar input.

A related, complementary goal — currently hampered by the same computational bottlenecks — is to complement traditional summary statistics (such as two- and three-point statistics) with more informative, data-driven summaries that better capture non-Gaussian features of evolved non-linear fields (e.g.~\citep{bairagi2025patchnethierarchicalapproachneural,Borg,lanzieri2025optimalneuralsummarisationfullfield,Bayer_2025}).

\section{The Cosmological Context}

\subsection{The expanding Universe}

Cosmology describes the origin, evolution, and large-scale structure (LSS) of the Universe as a whole. On the largest scales, the Universe is observed to be homogeneous and isotropic, an assumption known as the cosmological principle. Together with the assumption of a spatially flat geometry    strongly supported by measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB)~\citep{planck:2018}    this fixes the spacetime metric to the Friedmann  Robertson  Walker (FRW) form,
\begin{equation}
    \text{d}s^2 = -c^2\,\text{d}t^2 + a^2(t)\,\text{d}\bm{r}^2,
\end{equation}
where $a(t)$ is the scale factor describing the (relative) size of the Universe as a function of cosmic time $t$, conventionally normalized to $a=1$ today. Inserting this metric into the Einstein field equations relates the expansion rate to the energy content of the Universe, giving the Friedmann equation,
\begin{equation}
    H(t) \equiv \frac{1}{a}\frac{\text{d}a}{\text{d}t} = H_0\sqrt{\Om\,a^{-3} + \Omega_\Lambda},
\end{equation}
where $H_0\equiv H(t=1)$ is the Hubble constant, $\Om$ is the matter density parameter (dark matter plus baryons), and $\Omega_\Lambda$ is the dark-energy density parameter, with $\Om+\Omega_\Lambda=1$ for a flat Universe (radiation is a negligible contribution at the late times relevant to this thesis, and is dropped here for clarity). At early times, when $\Om a^{-3}\gg\Omega_\Lambda$, the Universe is matter dominated and $a(t)\propto t^{2/3}$; at late times, as $\Omega_\Lambda$ comes to dominate, the expansion instead accelerates towards $a(t)\propto e^{H_0 t}$. Current measurements give $\Om=0.315\pm0.007$~\citep{planck:2018}, with late-time probes broadly consistent with this value, though not without some persistent tension.

\subsection{Growth of structure}

The Universe is not perfectly homogeneous: small primordial density perturbations, seeded during an early inflationary epoch, grow under gravitational instability into the large-scale structure observed today. It is convenient to describe these perturbations through the fractional matter overdensity,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(\bm{r},t) \equiv \frac{\rho(\bm{r},t) - \langle\rho(\bm{r},t)\rangle}{\langle\rho(\bm{r},t)\rangle},
\end{equation}
where $\langle\dots\rangle$ denotes an ensemble average. At times well after recombination and on sub-horizon scales, $\delta$ obeys, in Fourier space,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(a^2(t)\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\delta(\bm{k},t)\right) = 4\pi G\,a^2(t)\,\rho(t)\,\delta(\bm{k},t),
\end{equation}
sourced by the Poisson equation relating the gravitational potential $\Phi$ to $\delta$. Because this equation is linear in $\delta$, its solution factorizes into a part depending only on time and a part depending only on scale,
\begin{equation}
    \delta(\bm{k},a) = \frac{2k^2}{5\Om H_0^2}\,\mathcal{R}(\bm{k})\,T(k)\,D_+(a),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}(\bm{k})$ encodes the primordial (inflationary) fluctuations, $T(k)$ is the transfer function describing how different scales evolve differently around the epoch of matter-radiation equality, and $D_+(a)$ is the linear growth factor. The growth factor satisfies $D_+(a)\propto a$ during matter domination, and is suppressed at late times as dark-energy domination takes over and accelerated expansion begins to suppress further collapse.

\subsection{The power spectrum and Gaussianity}

The statistical properties of the density field are most commonly characterized through the (linear) matter power spectrum $\Pk$, defined via the two-point correlation function in Fourier space,
\begin{equation}
    (2\pi)^3\,\Pk\,\delta_\text{D}^3(\bm{k}+\bm{k}') = \langle\delta(\bm{k})\delta(\bm{k}')\rangle,
\end{equation}
with $\delta_\text{D}$ the Dirac delta function. The factorized form of $\delta(\bm{k},a)$ above implies $P_\text{Lin}(k,a)\propto T^2(k)\,D_+^2(a)$. A key property of the \emph{linear} density field is that, to very high precision, it is Gaussian: this is a direct consequence of the Gaussian initial conditions from inflation, preserved under linear gravitational evolution. For a Gaussian field, the power spectrum is a \emph{complete} statistical description every higher order correlator $\langle\delta(\bm{k}_1)\cdots\delta(\bm{k}_n)\rangle$ either vanishes (for odd $n$) or reduces to a sum of products of two-point functions (for even $n$), a consequence of Wick's theorem. The overall amplitude of the (linear) power spectrum is conventionally summarized by the parameter $\sige$, the root-mean-square overdensity in spheres of radius $8\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}$,
\begin{equation}
    \sige^2(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty \text{d}k\; k^2\, P_\text{Lin}(k,a)\, W^2(kR), \qquad R = 8\,h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc},
\end{equation}
with $W$ a top-hat filter in Fourier space. Together, $\Om$ and $\sige$ are two of the most fundamental parameters describing the amount and clustering strength of matter in the Universe, and much of observational cosmology including the probes discussed later in this Chapter is aimed at constraining them.

\subsection{The nonlinear scale}

The Gaussian, linear picture above is only valid while density fluctuations remain small, $|\delta|\ll1$. This breaks down once fluctuations become of order unity, defining a nonlinear wavenumber $k_\text{NL}$ through the dimensionless power spectrum,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^2(k_\text{NL}) \equiv \frac{k_\text{NL}^3}{2\pi^2}\,P_\text{Lin}(k_\text{NL}) \sim 1,
\end{equation}
which for present-day $\Lambda$CDM parameters occurs at $k_\text{NL}\sim0.2\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$~\citep{Takahashi:2012}. Beyond this scale, gravitational collapse drives the density field away from Gaussianity, producing the cosmic web of halos, filaments, and voids observed today; this is precisely the regime that the field-level machine learning methods developed in this thesis are designed to model.

\subsection{The Jeans scale}

It is worth being careful to distinguish $k_\text{NL}$, which marks where \emph{gravitational} clustering of matter becomes nonlinear, from a second, physically distinct scale relevant once baryons (ordinary gas) are considered: the Jeans scale, below which gas pressure rather than gravity controls whether a region collapses.

A simple, order of magnitude way to see where this scale comes from is to compare two timescales for a gas cloud of size $\lambda$ and density $\rho$: the time for it to collapse under its own gravity, and the time for a pressure (sound) wave to cross it and counteract that collapse. The gravitational free-fall time scales as
\begin{equation}
    t_\text{gravity} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{G\rho}},
\end{equation}
while the sound-crossing time is
\begin{equation}
    t_\text{sound} \sim \frac{\lambda}{c_s},
\end{equation}
where $c_s$ is the sound speed of the gas. Collapse can proceed only if gravity acts faster than pressure can respond, i.e.\ $t_\text{gravity} \lesssim t_\text{sound}$. Setting the two timescales equal defines the Jeans length,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_J \sim \frac{c_s}{\sqrt{G\rho}},
\end{equation}
or, as a wavenumber, $k_J\sim 2\pi/\lambda_J$. (A more careful derivation, linearizing the fluid equations with pressure included, gives the same scaling with an $\mathcal{O}(1)$ prefactor~\citep{peacock_cosmological_1998}.) Below this scale, thermal/pressure support resists collapse; above it, self-gravity wins and the gas can collapse along with the dark matter.

The Jeans scale and the nonlinear scale $k_\text{NL}$ therefore answer different questions    one is about gravitational clustering of matter in general (relevant for dark matter, which has no pressure support), the other about whether \emph{gas} specifically can collapse into that clustered structure    and both are relevant once modeling baryonic tracers such as neutral hydrogen. This is one of the reasons, elaborated on later in this Chapter, that hydrodynamical simulations (rather than dark-matter-only $N$-body simulations) are needed to model such tracers realistically.

\subsection{Beyond linear theory: a brief note on perturbation theory}

Extending the description of structure formation beyond the linear regime is, in principle, possible order-by-order in what is known as cosmological perturbation theory, which introduces higher-order corrections to the power spectrum (e.g.\ at ``1-loop'' order) and to new statistics such as the bispectrum,
\begin{equation}
    (2\pi)^3\,B(k_1,k_2,k_3)\,\delta_\text{D}^3(\bm{k}_1+\bm{k}_2+\bm{k}_3) = \langle\delta(\bm{k}_1)\delta(\bm{k}_2)\delta(\bm{k}_3)\rangle,
\end{equation}
which vanishes identically for a Gaussian field but carries additional cosmological information once non-linearities become significant~\citep{sefu06}. In practice, however, this perturbative approach becomes rapidly more complex at higher orders, and even at next-to-leading order it already departs from full $N$-body simulation results by an order of magnitude on the mildly non-linear scales relevant to this work~\citep{Chudaykin_2020}. This breakdown of analytic methods precisely on the scales that carry the most cosmological information    rather than any detailed treatment of perturbation theory itself, which is not the focus of this thesis is the key motivation for turning to simulations, and ultimately to the machine-learning-based, field-level approaches developed in later Chapters.

\section{21cm Cosmology and Intensity Mapping}

\subsection{The hyperfine transition and spin temperature}

Neutral hydrogen (HI) has a ground-state hyperfine splitting, caused by the interaction between the magnetic moments of the proton and electron, whose transition corresponds to a rest-frame wavelength of 21\,cm (frequency $\nu_{21}=1420.4$\,MHz). The relative population of the two hyperfine levels is described by the spin temperature $T_S$, defined through the Boltzmann relation between the level populations,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{n_1}{n_0} = 3\,e^{-T_\star/T_S}
\end{equation}
where $n_1$ and $n_0$ are the populations of the upper and lower hyperfine levels, the factor of 3 is the ratio of their statistical weights, and $T_\star\equiv h\nu_{21}/k_B\approx0.068$\,K is the temperature corresponding to the transition energy.

The spin temperature is set by a competition between three effects: absorption/stimulated emission of CMB photons, which drives $T_S$ towards the CMB temperature $T_\text{CMB}$; collisions between hydrogen atoms (and, at high redshift, with free electrons), which drive $T_S$ towards the kinetic temperature of the gas $T_K$; and scattering of Lyman-$\alpha$ photons, which does the same via the Wouthuysen Field effect~\citep{Furlanetto:2006}. In the regimes relevant to this thesis, at the redshifts and densities probed by post-reionization 21\,cm intensity mapping, collisions dominate and are efficient enough that $T_S$ closely tracks $T_K$, decoupling it from $T_\text{CMB}$.

The observable 21\,cm signal is the differential brightness temperature of a patch of sky relative to the CMB, which can be written schematically as
\begin{equation}
    \delta T_b(z) \approx \bar{T}_b(z)\,\big(1+\delta_\text{HI}\big)\left(1 - \frac{T_\text{CMB}}{T_S}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\delta_\text{HI}$ is the HI density contrast and $\bar{T}_b(z)$ is a redshift-dependent normalization set by the mean HI abundance and the background cosmology~\citep{Furlanetto:2006}. In the post-reionization regime relevant to the intensity mapping experiments discussed later in this Chapter, $T_S\gg T_\text{CMB}$ to good approximation, so the factor in parentheses tends to unity and the 21\,cm signal is seen in emission, tracing $(1+\delta_\text{HI})$ directly. 

\subsection{Self-shielding and optical depth}

Not all neutral hydrogen contributes equally to the observable signal: in the presence of an ionizing ultraviolet background, gas at low density is highly
ionized, while sufficiently dense gas self-shields against this background and remains predominantly neutral. This transition is governed by the local optical depth to ionizing photons, and is commonly modeled in simulations via the fitting formula of~\citet{Rahmati_2013}, which prescribes the local
photoionization rate as a function of gas density:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{phot}}}{\Gamma_{\mathrm{UVB}}}
    = (1 - f)\!\left[1 + \left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{H}}}{n_0}\right)^{\!\beta}\right]^{\alpha_1}
    + f\!\left[1 + \frac{n_{\mathrm{H}}}{n_0}\right]^{\alpha_2},
    \label{eq:rahmati}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{UVB}}$ is the uniform UV background rate, $n_0$ is a characteristic self-shielding density threshold that depends on redshift and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{UVB}}$, and $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, $\beta$, $f$
are parameters calibrated against full radiative transfer simulations. At low densities ($n_{\mathrm{H}} \ll n_0$), the gas sees the full background and is highly ionized; at high densities ($n_{\mathrm{H}} \gg n_0$), $\Gamma_{\mathrm{phot}}$ is strongly suppressed and the gas remains predominantly neutral. The neutral fraction itself is then obtained by solving the ionization equilibrium between photoionization, collisional ionization, and recombination at each resolution element. This self-shielding prescription is
what allows a simulation, which by default tracks total gas (and dark matter) density, to be converted into a physically meaningful HI field for comparison
with 21\,cm observations, rather than simply assuming all gas is neutral.

At the level of the observed signal itself, the post-reionization 21\,cm line is generally an optically thin tracer: the probability that an emitted photon is re-absorbed before escaping is small, so the simple, linear relation between $\delta T_b$ and $\delta_\text{HI}$ above holds without needing full radiative transfer. This is a convenient simplification exploited throughout the modeling approaches discussed below, though it is worth noting explicitly since it is not guaranteed to hold in all regimes (e.g.\ deep inside very dense, self-shielded structures).

\subsection{Intensity mapping}

Rather than individually detecting and cataloguing galaxies, intensity mapping (IM) measures the aggregate, unresolved 21\,cm emission from all of the HI within a given angular pixel and frequency channel, effectively treating the sky as a continuous 3D field rather than a catalogue of discrete sources. This has two main advantages over traditional galaxy redshift surveys: it does not require individually resolving faint sources, allowing much larger comoving volumes and higher redshifts to be surveyed in a given observing time~\citep{Villaescusa-Navarro:2018}; and the observed frequency directly encodes the redshift of the emitting gas via the cosmological redshifting of the 21\,cm line, providing three-dimensional maps without the need for spectroscopic follow-up of individual objects~\citep[e.g.][]{Bharadwaj:2000,Battye:2004,Chang:2007}. The price paid for this efficiency is that the signal is dominated by bright astrophysical and instrumental foregrounds (Galactic synchrotron emission, extragalactic point sources, and radio-frequency interference) that must be modeled and removed, and that individual objects can no longer be identified   only the aggregate field. Cross-correlating IM maps with an independent, overlapping galaxy survey is a standard and powerful strategy for both validating the recovered HI signal and mitigating foreground contamination, since foregrounds are not expected to correlate with the (independently observed) galaxy positions.

\subsection{Modeling the HI field}

Translating between the underlying matter distribution and the observable HI field that intensity mapping probes requires a model connecting the two, and three broad approaches exist, each already introduced briefly in the current draft and expanded on here.

\paragraph{Halo models.} Halo-model approaches assign HI to dark matter halos according to a prescribed HI halo mass relation $M_\text{HI}(M)$~\citep{padmanabhan_hi_2023}, often calibrated against observations of damped Lyman-$\alpha$ systems, both from the halo-model side~\citep{Padmanabhan_2016} and observationally~\citep{Dev_2023,Obuljen_2019}. This approach is computationally cheap and can be applied to very large volumes, but relies on a simplified, effective description of a process that is, in reality, governed by detailed gas physics within and around individual halos.

\paragraph{Perturbation-theory-based modeling.} As discussed earlier in this Chapter, standard perturbation theory can in principle be extended to describe biased tracers such as HI~\citep{obuljen23}, but is subject to the same breakdown on mildly non-linear and non-linear scales as the dark matter perturbation theory discussed above, and is not treated in further analytic detail in this thesis.

\paragraph{Hydrodynamical simulations.} Full hydrodynamical simulations solve for the gas dynamics directly (subject to the self-shielding and other subgrid prescriptions discussed further in the next Section), capturing small-scale astrophysical processes   star formation, feedback, radiative transfer   that the two approaches above must instead approximate~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}. This comes at substantially higher computational cost, as quantified in the Simulation Bottleneck discussion later in this Chapter, and is the primary motivation, throughout this thesis, for developing fast machine learning based surrogates that retain hydrodynamical simulation fidelity at a fraction of the cost.

Beyond these three established approaches, a growing body of work instead learns the HI matter mapping directly from simulations in a data-driven way, without prescribing a specific functional form for $M_\text{HI}(M)$ in advance~\citep[e.g.][]{hitz2025fastsimulationcosmologicalneutral,li2022theoreticalmodelsatomichydrogen,spinelli_atomic_2020}. These forward-modeling approaches extend predictions to large volumes by applying calibrated prescriptions for the HI halo connection, but still fall short of learning the full field-level mapping from simulations   the specific gap that the methods developed in this thesis address.

\section{Galaxy Surveys}

Alongside 21\,cm intensity mapping, galaxy surveys remain the most established observational route to the large scale structure, and it is worth briefly reviewing the two dominant approaches   spectroscopic redshift surveys and weak gravitational lensing   both to contextualize 21\,cm IM among the broader observational landscape and because cross-correlation with these surveys is a recurring theme, and an explicit long term goal, of this thesis.

\subsection{Redshift surveys and baryon acoustic oscillations}

Spectroscopic redshift surveys measure the three dimensional positions of large numbers of galaxies by combining their angular position on the sky with a redshift obtained from an optical spectrum, directly mapping out the galaxy distribution as a tracer of the underlying matter field. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) and its successors the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS)~\citep{dawson_baryon_2013,BOSS:2016} and its extension eBOSS have produced some of the most precise galaxy clustering measurements to date, and the ongoing Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey is extending this approach to substantially larger volumes and higher redshifts~\citep{DESIde}.

A key feature exploited by these surveys is the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) signal: acoustic waves in the pre recombination photon-baryon plasma imprint a characteristic scale on the late time galaxy distribution, corresponding to the sound horizon at recombination. Because this physical scale is known precisely from CMB physics, its apparent size in a galaxy survey acts as a ``standard ruler'', providing a geometric measurement of the expansion history that is largely insensitive to the details of non linear structure formation   in contrast to the shape of the full power spectrum, which is sensitive to precisely the non-linear physics discussed earlier in this Chapter. A second, complementary signal available in the same data is redshift space distortions (RSD): peculiar velocities along the line of sight distort the observed (redshift  space) galaxy positions relative to their true, real space positions, providing a measurement of the growth rate of structure and thus an additional handle on gravity and the growth factor $D_+(a)$ introduced earlier in this Chapter~\citep[e.g.][]{Ross:2014}.

\subsection{Weak gravitational lensing}

A complementary approach, sensitive to the total matter distribution (dark and baryonic) rather than only to the (biased) tracer traced by galaxy positions, is weak gravitational lensing. Light from distant background galaxies is very slightly deflected and distorted sheared by the intervening large scale structure along the line of sight; while this distortion is far too small to detect in any single galaxy image, it can be measured statistically from the coherent alignment of many background galaxy shapes, a signal known as cosmic shear. Major ongoing and recent lensing surveys include the Dark Energy Survey (DES)~\citep{abbott:2018} and the Kilo-Degree Survey (KiDS)~\citep{Hildebrandt:2017}, with substantially larger and deeper surveys upcoming: Euclid~\citep{Euclid:2019clj,euclid_collaboration_et_al_euclid_2020,scaramella_euclid_2022} and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST)~\citep{Ivezic_2019}.

Because weak lensing probes the total matter distribution directly, rather than a specific luminous or 21\,cm tracer, cross-correlating a lensing survey with 21\,cm intensity maps (or with any of the tracers discussed elsewhere in this Chapter) provides a way to isolate genuine cosmological signal from tracer specific systematics and astrophysical uncertainties that do not correlate between independent probes   directly analogous to the galaxy IM cross correlation strategy already discussed for foreground mitigation. Cross correlations between weak lensing and post reionization neutral hydrogen probes have already been explored as a route to constraining dark energy~\citep{Dash:2020}, and this kind of multi tracer cross correlation, extended to the field level using the machine learning tools developed later in this thesis, is precisely the long term goal motivating the joint dark matter and halo, and dark matter and baryonic field, emulation approaches presented in the following Chapters.



\section{Current and Upcoming Experiments}

The theoretical picture developed above is being tested by a rapidly growing set of 21\,cm intensity mapping experiments, ranging from currently operating pathfinders to next-generation facilities still under construction.

\subsection{21cm intensity mapping instruments}

The Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) is a transit interferometer without moving parts, covering the frequency range corresponding to $0.8\lesssim z\lesssim2.5$~\citep{Bandura:2014gwa}; CHIME has already reported a direct detection of cosmological 21\,cm emission~\citep{CHIMEdetection}, a major milestone for interferometric 21\,cm IM. Related interferometric instruments include CHORD~\citep{thechordcollaboration2026overviewcanadianhydrogenobservatory}, a next-generation Canadian array currently under construction and designed to substantially improve on CHIME's sensitivity and frequency coverage, and HIRAX, a planned array of $\sim$1000 six-metre dishes to be sited in South Africa, targeting baryon acoustic oscillations over a wide sky area and redshift range $0.8\lesssim z\lesssim2.5$~\citep{Newburgh:2016mwi}; as of this writing, HIRAX remains in the dish deployment and construction phase, with cosmological results still forthcoming. On the single-dish side, the Green Bank Telescope (GBT) was used for some of the earliest cross-correlation detections of the 21\,cm signal~\citep{Masui2013,Wolz2022}, and the Five-hundred-metre Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) in China, the largest single-dish radio telescope in the world, is forecast to deliver competitive intensity mapping constraints once dedicated IM surveys are carried out~\citep{Hu:2019okh}.

\subsection{MeerKAT and MeerKLASS}

MeerKAT, a 64-dish interferometer in South Africa and an SKA precursor, has become one of the most productive current facilities for single-dish 21\,cm intensity mapping through its MeerKAT Large Area Synoptic Survey (MeerKLASS) programme~\citep{Santos:2017,meerklass_deepfield_2025}. Substantial effort has gone into the calibration and foreground-mitigation pipeline needed to extract a cosmological signal from single-dish autocorrelation data~\citep{Wang:2021,irfan2022}, culminating in a first cross-correlation detection of the 21\,cm signal with WiggleZ galaxies at $7.7\sigma$ significance~\citep{Cunnington:2022uzo}. More recently, deeper MeerKLASS observations, cross-correlated with an independent spectroscopic galaxy sample, have pushed this kind of measurement to greater statistical significance and to new redshift ranges, representing the deepest single-dish HI intensity maps obtained to date (see the bibkey note above for the specific reference to add). Taken together, these results mark a genuine milestone for the field, demonstrating that single-dish 21\,cm intensity mapping can deliver robust cosmological cross-correlation detections ahead of the full SKA era.

\subsection{The Square Kilometre Array Observatory}

Radio cosmology is also one of the flagship science drivers of the Square Kilometre Array Observatory (SKAO),\footnote{\url{https://www.skao.int/}} which will ultimately comprise two complementary telescopes: SKA-Low, located in Western Australia and optimized for lower radio frequencies (and correspondingly higher redshifts, probing the epoch of reionization and beyond), and SKA-Mid, located in South Africa (at the same site as MeerKAT, which will be incorporated into the array), covering higher frequencies corresponding to the lower-redshift, post-reionization Universe. Operated in single-dish (autocorrelation) mode, SKA-Mid is expected to conduct 21\,cm intensity mapping surveys across cosmologically relevant volumes out to $z\sim3$~\citep{Santos:2015,Bacon:2018}, directly extending the redshift range already being explored by its MeerKAT/MeerKLASS precursor. Both SKA-Low and SKA-Mid are currently under construction, with phased science operations expected to begin as construction and commissioning progress; the results already obtained by CHIME and MeerKAT/MeerKLASS, discussed above, serve as important pathfinders and proofs of concept for the much larger surveys the SKAO is ultimately designed to carry out.

Finally, it is worth noting that several of the galaxy-survey instruments already discussed earlier in this Chapter (DESI, Euclid, LSST/Rubin) are contemporaries of these 21\,cm facilities, and are expected to substantially overlap with them in sky coverage and observing epoch, further motivating the kind of cross-correlation and multi-tracer approaches that this thesis is ultimately aimed at supporting.



\section{From Landscape to Bottleneck}

The preceding sections have laid out, in turn, the theoretical machinery used to describe the cosmological matter and HI fields, the physics of the 21\,cm signal itself and the modeling strategies used to predict it, the simulation methods needed once that modeling moves beyond the reach of linear theory and perturbation theory, the complementary galaxy-survey probes that trace the same large-scale structure through independent tracers, and the current and upcoming instruments -- from CHIME and MeerKAT/MeerKLASS to the SKAO -- that are now turning all of this theoretical machinery into an observational programme.

A common thread runs through this landscape: in every case, the most physically complete description -- full hydrodynamical simulations, resolving gravity, gas dynamics, and the subgrid astrophysics of star formation and feedback -- is also the most computationally expensive one, precisely in the small-scale, non-linear regime ($k\gtrsim k_{\rm NL}$) where the wealth of new instruments described above is beginning to deliver data. Perturbative and halo-based approaches remain analytically tractable in this regime, but only by trading away exactly the physical content -- baryonic feedback, self-shielded HI, small-scale non-linear clustering -- that upcoming surveys are most sensitive to. This tension, between the fidelity of hydrodynamical simulations and their cost at the scale required for modern inference, is the subject of the following section.
