\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:emu_intro}

Upcoming large scale structure (LSS) surveys will map galaxy positions and
neutral hydrogen (HI) intensity over volumes large enough that statistical
noise ceases to be the limiting factor in cosmological parameter inference;
instead, the accuracy of the forward model connecting cosmological and
astrophysical parameters to the observed tracer fields becomes the
bottleneck. That mapping is governed by non-linear gravitational collapse
and by baryonic feedback processes (supernova and AGN feedback in
particular) that can only be captured through full hydrodynamical
simulations~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_camels_2022}. Traditional inference
pipelines sidestep this cost by compressing the field into a summary
statistic, usually the power spectrum, and assuming a Gaussian likelihood for
it. This is tractable, but discards the non-Gaussian information generated by
non-linear structure formation, an effect that grows with the degree of
non-Gaussianity and depends on the scales and tracers considered.

Simulation-based inference (SBI) removes the need for an explicit
likelihood by training a neural density estimator directly on
simulation--parameter pairs, and has become an increasingly common tool in
cosmology precisely because forward simulations, not tractable likelihoods,
are what is naturally available. Its main practical limitation is that it is
simulation-hungry: field-level SBI in particular requires simulation budgets
that are difficult to reach with full hydrodynamical suites such as
\CAMELS~\citep{villaescusa-navarro_camels_2022}, which sample the
cosmological and astrophysical parameter space with only $\mathcal{O}(10^3)$
runs. \emph{Emulators} address this bottleneck by learning the mapping from
cheap, approximate dark matter-only simulations (or directly from
parameters) to the corresponding baryonic observables, replacing the
expensive hydrodynamical step with a network evaluation that costs
milliseconds rather than CPU-hours. This is the role the emulator plays in
the present chapter: it is not itself the science goal, but the component
that makes a full-field, multi-tracer SBI analysis computationally
feasible at all.

It is worth situating this approach relative to the rest of the thesis.
Chapter~\ref{ch:dps} develops diffusion-based posterior sampling as a route
to field-level inference, drawing samples from an implicit posterior over
the dark matter field itself. The work presented here follows a
complementary strategy: rather than sampling the DM field posterior directly,
we use a deterministic, conditional emulator to generate tracer fields from
DM inputs, and perform inference with normalizing-flow-based neural
posterior estimation (NPE) on either summary statistics or a learned latent
representation of those fields. Both approaches share the same underlying
motivation, extracting the cosmological information lost when a field is
compressed to a power spectrum, but they trade off differently between
expressiveness, computational cost, and the kind of posterior they target
(a full field posterior in one case, a marginal posterior on a handful of
cosmological parameters in the other).

The proof of concept study summarized in this chapter combines three
ingredients: (i) fast, approximate dark matter simulations from \FastPM; (ii)
the \HGL emulator, which paints galaxy number-count and HI brightness-temperature
fields onto those DM maps; and (iii) an NPE pipeline that infers $\Om$ and
$\sige$ either from the two tracers' power spectra or from a full 3D
field-level representation, single-tracer or jointly. The rest of the
chapter is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:emu_pipeline} gives a
high-level overview of the pipeline. Section~\ref{sec:emu_dmmaps} describes
the DM simulations and the target galaxy/HI fields used to train the
emulator. Sections~\ref{sec:emu_arch} and~\ref{sec:emu_train} describe the
\HGL architecture and its training in detail. This is the core contribution
of the author to this work. Section~\ref{sec:emu_sbi} summarizes, briefly,
how the emulated fields are subsequently used for SBI.
Section~\ref{sec:emu_results} reports results, first on the fidelity of the
emulator itself (Section~\ref{sec:emu_val}), then on the downstream
cosmological constraints it enables (Section~\ref{sec:emu_downstream}).
Section~\ref{sec:emu_discussion} discusses limitations and future
directions.

\section{Pipeline overview}
\label{sec:emu_pipeline}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{chapters/ch5_emulator/emulator2.pdf}
    \caption{High-level overview of the pipeline described in this chapter. Approximate dark matter maps generated with \FastPM are passed through \HGL, the Attention Unet emulator, to produce galaxy and HI fields conditioned on cosmological and astrophysical parameters. These emulated fields are then either compressed to a power spectrum or fed to a convolutional encoder, and in both cases used to train a normalizing-flow-based NPE model that infers the posterior on $\Om$ and $\sige$.}
    \label{fig:emulator_pipeline_sketch}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:emulator_pipeline_sketch} sketches the full pipeline. Starting
from a cosmology $\theta_{\mathcal{C}} = \{\Om, \sige\}$, we generate an
approximate DM density field at $z=0$ with \FastPM. The \HGL emulator maps
this DM field, together with a choice of astrophysical parameters
$\theta_{\mathcal{A}}$, onto galaxy number-density and HI brightness-temperature
fields (Sections~\ref{sec:emu_dmmaps}--\ref{sec:emu_train}). These emulated
fields are the only place where the expensive, hydrodynamics-dependent physics
enters the pipeline; everything downstream of them, the power spectrum
extraction, the convolutional compression, and the normalizing flow, only
ever sees emulator output (Section~\ref{sec:emu_sbi}). All results in this
chapter refer to observables at $z=0$ in the plane-parallel approximation.

\section{dark matter maps and target observables}
\label{sec:emu_dmmaps}

\paragraph{Approximate dark matter simulations.}
DM-only maps are generated with \FastPM~\citep{feng_fastpm_2016}, a particle-mesh
code that is several orders of magnitude cheaper than a full N-body run
while still capturing the non-linear growth of structure relevant on the
scales of interest here. We generate cubes at two box sizes, $L=25\Mpch$
($128^3$ particles and mesh, saved at $64^3$ resolution) and $L=50\Mpch$
($256^3$ particles and mesh, saved at $128^3$ resolution), so that both box
sizes share the same voxel resolution. Initial conditions are set at $z=9$
with 2LPT and evolved to $z=0$ with 10 \FastPM steps, at a cost of
$\sim 0.3$ CPU-hours per realization. For the emulator training set, we
reuse the same random seeds and linear power spectrum provided by the
\CAMELS Latin Hypercube (LH) suite, since \FastPM's random number generator
is compatible with the \texttt{N-GenIC} generator used by \CAMELS; this lets
us pair each \FastPM DM cube with the corresponding hydrodynamical galaxy/HI
target on a like-for-like basis. When emulating new maps outside the
training set (i.e.\ for SBI), the linear power spectrum is instead obtained
from the \texttt{CLASS} Boltzmann solver for arbitrary seeds and parameter
draws.

\paragraph{CAMELS hydrodynamical targets.}
The emulator's target fields are drawn from the IllustrisTNG LH suite of
\CAMELS~\citep{nelson2021illustristngsimulationspublicdata}, which varies two
cosmological parameters ($\Om, \sige$) together with four astrophysical
feedback-strength parameters
($A_{\rm SN1}, A_{\rm AGN1}, A_{\rm SN2}, A_{\rm AGN2}$) governing supernova
and AGN feedback, over the same prior ranges already introduced in
Table~\ref{tab:lh_params}. Two tracers are extracted from each snapshot:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Galaxy number counts.} Sub-halos above $10^9\,M_\odot/h$
    in mass with non-zero stellar mass are painted onto a $64^3$ grid with
    a cloud-in-cell (CIC) scheme, smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of radius
    $R=0.2\Mpch$, and low values are clipped to $10^{-4}$ to stabilize
    training.
    \item \textbf{HI brightness temperature.} For each gas particle $i$ we
    combine the neutral hydrogen fraction $\xHI$ and gas mass to obtain the
    HI density,
    \begin{equation}
        \rho_{\mathrm{HI},i} = m_{g,i}\, X\, x_\mathrm{HI,i}
        \left(\frac{N_{\rm part}}{a L}\right)^{3}\ \left[\frac{\rm kg}{\rm m^3}\right],
    \end{equation}
    with $X=0.76$ the hydrogen mass fraction and $a$ the scale factor,
    following the self shielding prescription of
    \citet{villaescusa-navarro_ingredients_2018}. After interpolating onto a
    $64^3$ grid with CIC, this is converted to the post-reionization
    brightness temperature,
    \begin{equation}
        \Tb(\mathbf{x}) = 189\, h \left(\frac{H_0 (1+z)^2}{H(z)}\right)
        \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{HI}}(\mathbf{x})}{\rho_c}\ [\mathrm{mK}],
    \end{equation}
    and smoothed with the same $R=0.2\Mpch$ kernel as the galaxy field.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why two box sizes.}
The $25\Mpch$ box is what the \CAMELS LH suite provides, and is therefore
the only size at which paired DM/hydro training data exists. However, at
this box size the power spectrum is noticeably sensitive to cosmic variance:
realizations at fixed physical parameters but different random seeds can
yield visibly different power spectra, which introduces a degeneracy
between cosmology and realization noise. We therefore adopt $50\Mpch$ boxes
for the SBI stage to improve large scale mode sampling, while still training
the emulator itself at $25\Mpch$, since the LH suite is not available at the
larger box size (the available alternative, the SB35 suite, varies 30
astrophysical parameters simultaneously instead of 4, making it unsuitable
for this emulator). This choice, using an emulator trained at one box size
to generate maps at another, is only valid because the two box sizes share
the same voxel resolution. This of course is also the main limitation of the current pipeline, which is also discussed in Section~\ref{sec:emu_val}.

\section{The \HGL emulator: architecture}
\label{sec:emu_arch}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{chapters/ch5_emulator/pictures/emulator_architecture.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of the \HGL emulator. A DM map generated with
    \FastPM is passed through a conditional 3D U-Net to produce a galaxy or
    HI field, with the cosmological and astrophysical parameters injected
    at every residual block via FiLM conditioning.}
    \label{fig:emulator_architecture}
\end{figure}

The task of predicting a 3D galaxy or HI field from its DM counterpart is,
structurally, an image-to-image translation problem, and we address it with
a conditional 3D U-Net~\citep{ronneberger_u-net_2015},
which we refer to as \HGL (\HALOgen-like). This builds directly on the
\HALOgen architecture introduced in the author's earlier
work~\citep{mishra2025largefastaccuratehi}, but with a structural change that is the central
methodological contribution described here: \HALOgen was designed to emulate
fields at a single, fixed cosmology, whereas \HGL must generalize over both
cosmological parameters $\theta_{\mathcal{C}} = \{\Om, \sige\}$ and
astrophysical parameters
$\theta_{\mathcal{A}} = \{A_{\rm SN1}, A_{\rm AGN1}, A_{\rm SN2}, A_{\rm AGN2}\}$
at inference time, rather than requiring a separately trained network per
parameter point.

\paragraph{Backbone.}
The network follows a standard encoder--decoder U-Net layout with four
downsampling and four upsampling stages (Table~\ref{tab:emu_architecture}
and Figure~\ref{fig:emulator_architecture}). Each stage is a residual block
consisting of two 3D convolutions, each preceded by Group Normalization and
a non-linear activation; downsampling is performed with max pooling and
upsampling with trilinear interpolation. Skip connections concatenate
encoder features into the corresponding decoder stage at matching
resolution, which is what allows the network to recover high-frequency
structure that would otherwise be lost during downsampling. At the
bottleneck, a self attention layer lets the network model long-range,
non-local correlations between voxels, which is particularly relevant for
capturing large scale structure that a purely convolutional receptive field
would only see indirectly through depth.

\paragraph{Conditioning via FiLM.}
The mechanism that lets a single network generalize across parameters is
Feature-wise Linear Modulation
(FiLM)~\citep{perez2017filmvisualreasoninggeneral}. For a feature layer
$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D^3}$ inside a residual block, and a
conditioning vector $\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}$, FiLM applies a
channel-wise affine transformation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FiLM}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{c}) =
    \boldsymbol{\gamma}(\mathbf{c}) \odot \mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{c}),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ are
produced from $\mathbf{c}$ by a shared multi-layer perceptron,
$[\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\mathbf{c}), \boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{c})] =
f_\theta(\mathbf{c})$, and $\odot$ is an element-wise product across
channels. Two such transformations are applied per residual block (one per
convolution), so the number of $(\gamma, \beta)$ pairs at a given stage
scales with that stage's channel count. In this work, $\mathbf{c}$ is the
concatenation of cosmological and astrophysical parameters,
$\mathbf{c} = [\theta_{\mathcal{C}}, \theta_{\mathcal{A}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{6}$,
so the same physical parameters that define a \CAMELS simulation are what
condition every feature layer of the network. Replacing the fixed affine
parameters of a standard normalization layer with FiLM's
conditioning-dependent ones is what turns a single cosmology emulator such as
the original \HALOgen into one that can be queried at arbitrary points in
parameter space without retraining.

\paragraph{What the emulator can and cannot vary.}
Given a DM map $\mathcal{F}^{\rm DM}_{i}(\Omega_{m_i}, \sigma_{8_i})$ at cosmology $i$, the
network produces the corresponding galaxy or HI field at that same
cosmology, for any astrophysics $j$ of choice,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}^{\rm DM}_{i}(\Omega_{m_i}, \sigma_{8_i}) \:\: \xrightarrow[]{\text{U-Net}} \:\: \mathcal{F}^{\rm gal/HI}_{i}(\Omega_{m_i}, \sigma_{8_i}, {\rm A_{SN1_{j}},A_{AGN1_{j}},A_{SN2_{j}},A_{AGN2_{j}}}).
\end{equation}
Astrophysics can be varied freely at inference time because it is injected
purely through the FiLM conditioning; cosmology cannot, because it is
already baked into the input DM field itself through the linear power
spectrum used to generate it. In other words, the emulator paints
astrophysics onto a fixed gravitational skeleton, it cannot repaint a DM
field generated at one cosmology as if it belonged to another. This is a
structural constraint of the approach, not a training limitation, and it is
what makes the DM-map generation step (Section~\ref{sec:emu_dmmaps})
integral to the emulator rather than a separate pre-processing step.

\section{Emulator training}
\label{sec:emu_train}

\paragraph{Data splits.}
The $508$ \CAMELS LH seeds spanning the full cosmological and astrophysical
prior range are split $75\%/25\%$ into training and validation sets, with an
independent test set of $242$ seeds held out entirely. To increase the
effective size of the training set without introducing new simulations, each
training/validation cube is augmented with all $24$ possible axis-aligned
rotations, giving $508 \times 24 = 12{,}192$ cube instances; the test set is
left unrotated at $242$ cubes. Every map (input DM and target galaxy/HI) is
standard-scaled using statistics fit on its own training split, and each
network is trained on a single tracer channel at a time (i.e.\ separate
networks for galaxies and for HI).

\paragraph{Loss function.}
Following the quantile-regression approach used in the original \HALOgen
model, the emulator is trained with a sum of three quantile
losses,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \mathrm{QL}(y, \hat{y}; \tau_i), \qquad
    \mathrm{QL}(y, \hat{y}; \tau) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
    \max\big(\tau (y_j - \hat{y}_j),\, (\tau - 1)(y_j - \hat{y}_j)\big),
\end{equation}
where $y$ and $\hat y$ are the target and predicted voxel values. Rather
than regressing a single point estimate, this trains the network to
reproduce several quantiles of the conditional target distribution at once,
which improves robustness on the sparse, highly non-Gaussian galaxy field in
particular. We use $\tau \in \{0.45, 0.60, 0.75\}$ for HI and
$\tau \in \{0.45, 0.60, 0.80\}$ for galaxies, chosen empirically. During
training we checkpoint the model every time the loss improves, and select,
a posteriori, both the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss (best at
the pixel level) and the checkpoint with the smallest power-spectrum
residual on held-out validation maps. For the HI emulator these two
criteria pick the same checkpoint; for the galaxy emulator they do not,
which is itself informative, it means the epoch at which the network best
reproduces individual voxel values is not necessarily the epoch at which it
best reproduces the two point statistics of the field, and the two should be
tracked and reported separately rather than assuming pixel level accuracy
implies summary-statistic accuracy (or vice versa). Once trained, generating
a single $64^3$ map takes $\sim 10^{-2}\,$s on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, several
orders of magnitude faster than the hydrodynamical simulation it replaces.

\section{Downstream use: simulation-based inference}
\label{sec:emu_sbi}

The remainder of the pipeline, developed jointly with the co-authors, is
summarized here only to the extent needed to interpret the results in
Section~\ref{sec:emu_downstream}. Emulated galaxy and HI maps are used to
train a neural posterior estimator (NPE) that approximates
$p(\theta_{\mathcal C} \mid \mathbf{d})$ directly from
simulation--parameter pairs, modeling the posterior with a Masked
Autoregressive Flow
(MAF)~\citep{papamakarios_masked_2018}. Two data representations are compared: SBI on
the power spectrum $\Pk$ of the emulated fields (case $\mathcal{P}$, a
summary-statistics baseline), and SBI on a latent representation of the
full 3D (or, for comparison, axis-averaged 2D) field extracted by a
convolutional encoder trained jointly with the flow (case $\mathcal{M}$).
Both cases are run for each tracer individually and for the two tracers
combined, either fixing the astrophysical parameters to their fiducial
values or marginalizing over them. The purpose of this comparison, discussed
further below, is to isolate how much cosmological information is retained
(or lost) at each stage of compression, from a full field, down to a
latent vector, down to a power spectrum, given input fields whose fidelity is
itself set by the \HGL emulator described above.

degradation for the HI tracers, similar for the galaxy case. The most likely explanation for both these $50\Mpch$ cube is that larger
boxes contain larger scale modes, and correspondingly more massive halos,
than the network ever saw during training on smaller boxes; the emulator is
being asked to extrapolate into a regime it was never exposed to, not
merely to interpolate within its training distribution. We adopt this
configuration for the SBI stage regardless, since it is internally
consistent (both the SBI training and test sets are generated by the same
emulator, so the network's own biases are shared between them and do not,
by construction, bias the SBI comparison), but this is the clearest
limitation of the current emulator, and one that would need to be resolved,
most directly by training on paired DM/hydro data at $50\Mpch$ once such a
suite becomes available, before applying this pipeline to real survey data
or to maps generated by a different DM-only simulator (see
Section~\ref{sec:emu_discussion}).

It is worth spelling out why these particular gains appear, since the
pattern is not simply ``more information is always better.'' The power
spectrum is, by construction, a two point statistic: it is blind to any
information encoded in higher-order correlations of the field. Non-linear
gravitational evolution generates exactly this kind of higher-order,
non-Gaussian structure, and it is particularly sensitive to $\sige$, which
sets the amplitude of fluctuations and therefore the strength of mode
coupling between different scales. The convolutional encoder feeding the
3D$\mathcal{M}$ case is able to extract part of this non-Gaussian
information; the power spectrum, by definition, cannot. That the
2D$\mathcal{M}$ case fails to reproduce this gain is itself informative:
flattening the cube along the line of sight destroys precisely the
along-the-line structure that carries this extra information, so the
improvement is not simply a property of ``using a neural network'' but
specifically of preserving the field's full three-dimensional structure.

The multi-tracer gain has a complementary explanation, illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:posteriors_downstream_fixedastro_multi}. Galaxy number
counts are a sparse, shot-noise-dominated tracer built from halos spanning
a wide range of masses and bias factors, which mixes together objects that
trace the underlying dark matter field in different ways; HI brightness
temperature, by contrast, is a smoother, more continuous tracer with a more
direct dependence on the underlying density.

Combining the two through their cross correlation lets the inference
disentangle genuine cosmological signal from tracer specific effects (shot
noise, bias) that do not correlate between the two tracers, which is
precisely why $\mathcal{P}^{\rm g\times HI}$ and 3D$\mathcal{M}^{\rm g\times
HI}$ outperform either single tracer case on its own. None of this should
be read as a criticism of galaxy clustering as an observational probe in
general: real galaxy surveys additionally exploit physically motivated bias
models, much larger survey volumes, and observables such as redshift space
distortions that are absent from this idealized, box limited comparison.

\FloatBarrier

From the perspective of this chapter, the relevant point is narrower: none
of these downstream comparisons would be meaningful if the emulated fields
feeding them were not themselves accurate, at both the pixel and summary statistic
level. This is precisely
what Section~\ref{sec:emu_val} was intended to establish, that the \HGL
emulator reproduces both the power spectrum and the pixel level statistics
of the tracers it was trained on to good accuracy at $25\Mpch$, with
box-size extrapolation to $50\Mpch$ identified as the dominant residual
source of emulator-level systematic error. The downstream SBI comparison
in Table~\ref{tab:emu_fom} should therefore be read as a demonstration of
what becomes possible once a sufficiently accurate emulator removes the need
for a prohibitively large hydrodynamical simulation suite, rather than as an
independent validation of the emulator itself.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:emu_discussion}

The \HGL emulator generalizes the single-cosmology \HALOgen
architecture~\citep{mishra2025largefastaccuratehi} to arbitrary cosmological and astrophysical
parameters within the \CAMELS LH prior, at a training and inference cost
low enough to support a full SBI pipeline. Three limitations are worth
highlighting, since they define the natural next steps for this line of
work rather than being incidental to it.

First, and most importantly, generalization across box size is limited:
the emulator trained at $25\Mpch$ shows a visibly larger power-spectrum
residual when used to generate $50\Mpch$ maps
(Section~\ref{sec:emu_val}), most likely because the larger box exposes
large scale modes and massive halos absent from the smaller training
volumes. Because the current \CAMELS LH suite provides paired DM/hydro
simulations only at $25\Mpch$, this cannot be fully resolved without new
training data at larger box size; a supervised fine-tuning step, or a
loss term that explicitly penalizes large scale power-spectrum residuals,
would both be natural directions to explore in the meantime.

Second, the checkpoint selection issue noted in Section~\ref{sec:emu_train},
that the best pixel level checkpoint and the best power-spectrum checkpoint
do not coincide for the galaxy emulator, is a reminder that voxel wise loss
and two point function accuracy are not interchangeable diagnostics for a
field-level emulator, and that whichever one is optimized for should be
chosen deliberately based on the downstream use case (map realism vs.\
summary-statistic accuracy) rather than by default.

Third, the entire pipeline, training, validation, and the SBI test set
alike, is self consistent: everything is generated within the same emulator
framework, and no attempt is made here to test transfer to independently
generated maps (e.g.\ original \CAMELS hydrodynamical maps not used in
training, or maps from a different simulation suite altogether). Prior work
on out of distribution sensitivity in field-level SBI has shown that even
small numerical differences between simulation codes at fixed physical
parameters can bias inferred posteriors~\citep{Bayer_2025}; since an
emulator is, by construction, another source of such small-scale
discrepancies relative to the simulations it approximates, a dedicated
out of distribution study is a necessary step before this pipeline could be
applied to real observational data, or even to hydrodynamical simulations
outside the training suite.

Looking forward, the natural extensions are to retrain \HGL once a larger
paired training suite becomes available, to test transfer to independent
simulation codes and to real survey-like maps (including instrumental noise,
beams, and foregrounds for the HI tracer, and realistic selection functions
for the galaxy tracer), and to extend the redshift range beyond the $z=0$
snapshot considered here. More broadly, this combination of emulator and neural posterior estimation approach and the diffusion-based posterior sampling framework of Chapter~\ref{ch:dps}
represent two complementary routes to the same underlying goal: recovering
the cosmological information that summary statistics discard, in a
computational regime where full hydrodynamical simulations, run at the
scale a full posterior or a well-sampled training set requires, remain out of reach.
