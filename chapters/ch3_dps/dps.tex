\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:dps_intro}



The intergalactic medium (IGM) at high redshift holds the majority of the Universe's baryonic content, and on megaparsec scales its distribution traces the underlying dark matter field shaped by gravitational instability~\citep{Meiksin_2009,McQuinn_2016,Bi_1997}. The Lyman-$\alpha$ (Ly$\alpha$) forest , the pattern of absorption imprinted by intervening neutral hydrogen on the spectra of background quasars and galaxies , remains the most direct observational window onto this diffuse structure~\citep{Gunn_peterson,Lynds_1971,Williger_2000}. For over two decades, sufficiently dense grids of background sightlines have made it possible to move beyond one dimensional flux statistics and reconstruct the IGM tomographically in three dimensions~\citep{caucci2008recoveringtopologyigmz2,Lee_2014,Lee_2018}, a program now being extended by surveys such as DESI Ly$\alpha$ QSO, WEAVE-QSO, and Subaru-PFS/CLAMATO/LATIS, and, in the coming decade, by ELT/MOSAIC, which will push sightline separations down toward $1\Mpch$. At these finer separations, the reconstruction problem becomes increasingly sensitive to the baryonic physics, pressure smoothing, thermal broadening, and radiative feedback, that decouples the IGM from the pure dark matter field below $\sim 1\Mpch$~\citep{schaye_2000,Viel_2002,nasir2017effect}, motivating reconstruction methods that can go beyond the linear, large scale regime.

Classical tomographic reconstruction has relied on Wiener filtering or Gaussian process interpolation of the sparse sightline data~\citep{Lee_2014,Lee_2018}, which is efficient but smooths out precisely the small scale, non linear structure that upcoming surveys are designed to resolve. More recent semi-analytic approaches, notably TARDIS~\citep{horowitz_2019,horowitz_2021}, improve on this by embedding a physically motivated prior, nonlinear structure evolved forward with FastPM~\citep{feng_fastpm_2016}, inside an iterative maximum-likelihood scheme, using the Fluctuating Gunn-Peterson Approximation (FGPA) as the forward model linking density to absorption. This is a more faithful treatment of gravitational nonlinearity, but it inherits the limitations of FGPA at small scales and is computationally expensive, since every posterior sample requires re-running the gravity solver.

The last few years have seen deep generative models offer a third route: rather than approximating the forward problem or the prior analytically, learn one or both directly from hydrodynamical simulations. Variational autoencoders (VAE), GANs, Transformers and diffusion models have all been used to emulate non-linear cosmological fields. \citet{Moriwaki_2025} have applied generative Transformers to generate galaxy properties which enable fast generation of Line intensity maps and and redshift survey catalogs. \citet{Horowitz_2022} showed that a VAE trained on simulations can learn the mapping between dark matter and baryonic tracers directly, without any FGPA-like approximation. This is the paradigm adopted by DeepCHART~\citep{Maitra_2026}, developed and trained on the same $z=2.5$, $(40\,h^{-1}\mathrm{cMpc})^3$ Sherwood hydrodynamical simulations, also used throughout this chapter. DeepCHART uses a 3D U-Net VAE to map sparse Ly$\alpha$ forest sightlines, optionally combined with coeval galaxy positions, directly onto the 3D dark matter density field in a single amortized forward pass, mapping dark matter fields at a transverse sightline separation of $d_\perp \simeq 2.4\,h^{-1}\mathrm{cMpc}$ matched to the Subaru-PFS Deep survey.

\section{Novelty: Generative prior}
DeepCHART and the approach developed in this chapter are fundamentally different. An amortized VAE such as DeepCHART is trained end to end on paired (sightline, $\dmLOS$) data for a specific pairing of survey geometry and noise model; once trained, inference is near instantaneous, but the learned mapping is strongly coupled with the forward model used during training, so a new sightline density, or instrumental resolution requires retraining, and the network delivers a single deterministic point estimate of $\dmLOS$ per input. This work instead builds on the diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) framework~\citep{chung_diffusion_2023}, in which a diffusion model learns an unconditional generative prior over the baryonic and $\dmLOS$ fields themselves, trained once, without any paired flux data, and independently of any particular observational setup. At inference time this prior is combined with an explicit, differentiable forward model through a likelihood guidance term added to the reverse diffusion process. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:dps_formulation}, this decoupling is precisely what lets a single pretrained prior be redeployed across different observational setups without retraining, yielding genuine posterior samples rather than point estimates.

\vspace{\baselineskip}
In this chapter we introduce the diffusion posterior sampling (DPS, Section~\ref{sec:dps_theory}) framework to this thesis and apply it to the specific inverse problem of Lyman-$\alpha$ tomography. We train a variational diffusion model (VDM) prior directly on five physical fields, dark matter density ($\dmLOS$), gas density ($\gasLOS$), neutral hydrogen fraction ($\HILOS$), temperature ($T$), and peculiar velocity ($\vpec$), extracted from the same Sherwood hydrodynamical simulations underlying DeepCHART, and pair it with a fully differentiable, GPU-vectorized Voigt profile forward model that maps these fields onto synthetic Ly$\alpha$ forest spectra. Posterior samples of the joint 3D field are then drawn by guiding the reverse diffusion process with the gradient of the data likelihood at each denoising step, following the DPS formalism, with a scheduled guidance strength $\zeta$ added to stabilize sampling in three dimensions. A notable feature of this forward model is that $\dmLOS$ itself never enters the optical depth calculation directly, only $\gasLOS$, $\HILOS$, $T$, and $\vpec$ do, so any recovery of the dark matter field is mediated entirely by correlations the prior has learned between dark matter and the gas phase tracers, rather than by a direct likelihood constraint. This makes the dark matter reconstruction a genuine test of what the learned prior encodes about structure formation.

The following chapter is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lya-data-prep} describes the construction of the 3D training dataset from the Sherwood simulations. Section~\ref{sec:dps_architecture} details the architecture and training of the variational diffusion prior, together with the frozen VAE used in the latent formulation. Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory} presents the differentiable Voigt forward model. Section~\ref{sec:dps_theory} lays out the diffusion posterior sampling algorithm itself, the Bayesian decomposition into score and likelihood guidance terms, the Tweedie estimate, and memory-efficient gradient computation, as adapted to this three-dimensional, multi-channel setting. Section~\ref{sec:dps_results} presents reconstruction results, voxel wise fidelity for all five fields, recovery of the matter power spectrum. Lastly, Section~\ref{sec:dps_discussion} discusses the implications of these results, including what the free (likelihood blind) recovery of $\dmLOS$ implies about the prior, and directions for future work.

\subsection{Hydrodynamical simulations and line-of-sight fields}
\label{sec:lya-sims}

The training and validation data for this chapter are drawn from the same suite of Sherwood Relics hydrodynamical simulations \citep{bolton_sherwood_2017,puchwein_sherwood-relics_2023} used by \citet{Maitra_2026}, for direct comparability. These simulations are run with GADGET-3, self-consistently solving the coupled photoionization and thermal evolution equations of the gas under a specified ultraviolet background, within a periodic cube of comoving side $L_{\rm box}=40\Mpch$ populated with $2\times512^3$ dark matter and gas particles. Eight random-seed realizations are used for training and validating the diffusion prior and two are reserved for testing.

Rather than gridding the raw particle data directly, physical quantities are first evaluated along one dimensional sightlines threading the box, in a configuration matching Ly$\alpha$ forest tomography surveys. At redshift $z=2.5$, a sightline is discretized into $n_{\rm bins}$ velocity/position bins, and at each bin the local gas overdensity $\gasLOS$, temperature $T$, neutral hydrogen fraction $\HILOS$, and line of sight peculiar velocity $\vpec$ are obtained via standard SPH kernel interpolation from the neighboring gas particles,
\begin{equation}
f_i = \sum_j f_j\,\frac{m_j}{\rho_j}\,W\!\left(|\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j|,\,h_j\right),
\label{eq:sph-interp}
\end{equation}
where $f_j$, $m_j$, and $\rho_j$ are the quantity, mass, and density carried by particle $j$, and $W$ is the standard cubic-spline smoothing kernel \citep{sph1992,Maitra_2026}. The neutral hydrogen fraction $\HILOS$ and temperature $T$ carried by each gas particle are set on the fly during the simulation by GADGET-3's coupled photoionization and thermal evolution solver under the ultraviolet background of \citet{puchwein_sherwood-relics_2023}, including self-shielding corrections that suppress photoionization in dense gas; these particle-level values are then interpolated onto the sightline grid via Eq.~\ref{eq:sph-interp} exactly as $\gasLOS$ and $\vpec$ are. The dark matter overdensity $\dmLOS$ is obtained analogously from the dark matter particle distribution and gridded onto the same sightline geometry, but, as emphasized in Section~\ref{sec:dps_intro} , it is retained purely as a target field for the diffusion prior and never enters the optical depth calculation described in Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory}.

\section{The Variational Diffusion Prior}
\label{sec:dps_architecture}

Every other diffusion model in this thesis , \HALOgen\ and \LODI\ in Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi}, \FOLD\ in Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmofold}, and its posterior sampling extension in Chapter~\ref{ch:field_pos}, is a \emph{conditional} model, trained to map one field onto another (dark matter to halos, dark matter to gas temperature, stellar density to dark matter). The prior used in this chapter is different in kind: it is \emph{unconditional}, trained to model the joint distribution $p(\bm{x}_0)$ of the five-field state itself,
\begin{equation}
    \bm{x}_0 = \big(\dmLOS,\,\gasLOS,\,\HILOS,\,T,\,\vpec\big),
    \label{eq:dps_state}
\end{equation}
extracted from the Sherwood-Relics simulations (Section~\ref{sec:lya-sims}), with no conditioning information supplied at training time at all. This is precisely what makes the same trained network reusable, at inference time, against different sightline geometries, noise levels, or observational setups (Section~\ref{sec:dps_theory}): conditioning on a specific observation only ever enters through the likelihood-guidance term added to the reverse process, never through the network's own training.

\subsection{Multi-field representation}
\label{sec:dps_multifield}

The five physical fields of Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_state} are stacked as five channels of a single volumetric tensor, $\bm{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{5\times N_x\times N_y\times N_z}$, and modelled jointly by one network, rather than trained as five independent single-channel diffusion models. Treating them jointly helps with learning the implicit cross-field correlations during training, in particular between $\dmLOS$ and the four gas phase fields that actually enter the forward model of Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory}, that make the eventual likelihood-blind recovery of $\dmLOS$ possible at all (Section~\ref{sec:dps_theory}). Five independently trained priors would share no such correlations. The denoising network itself follows the same 3D U-Net with residual blocks and a self-attention bottleneck used throughout this thesis (Sections~\ref{sec:cnn_unet} and~\ref{sec:attention} of the Introduction; Section~\ref{sec:denoise_arch} of Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi}), adapted to use the correct dimensionality input and output..

\subsection{Latent diffusion and Anisotropic geometry}
\label{sec:dps_latent}

Because the training volume of $40^3 (\Mpch)^3$ is built from 1D sightlines threading the box (Section~\ref{sec:lya-sims}) rather than read directly off a native 3D grid, the natural tensor shape is highly anisotropic: The line of sight resolution is $\approx0.039\Mpch$(Ideally this would be set by the spectral resolution a survey would deliver), against a coarser resolution of $\approx0.31\Mpch$ along the two transverse directions (set by the physical separation between neighbouring sightlines). We denote this native shape $N_\parallel\times N_\perp\times N_\perp = 1024\times128\times128$. To get multiple samples and also due to memory reasons, we chunk these simulations into $1024\times32\times32$ sub volumes. Note that we do not chunk the LOS direction since we want the diffusion model to see the full line of sight structure in its training (since we realized that quantities such as $\vpec$ have strong long range correlations along LOS, and so the model needs to see them in a single pass to capture them). Running the reverse diffusion process directly on a tensor of this size is computationally demanding, especially if we want to use the Attention mechanism (Sec.\ref{sec:attention}). We therefore compress the $5\times1024\times32\times32$ training data into a latent representation which has a dimension of $8\times128\times16\times16$, resulting in a 20 times compression of the data. We denote this latent space by $\bm{x}_0^L$. This said compression is done via training a separate variational autoencoder (VAE), a general background of which have ben introduced in the introduction, in the section~\ref{sec:vae}. A Variational Diffusion model (\ref{sec:vdm}) is then trained to learn the prior of this latent space $\latent$. The the frozen VAE decoder is used only once, at the very end, to map the final denoised latent sample back to the native five-field volume.

\subsubsection{Encoding the line of sights}
\label{sec:VAE}

As mentioned, to perform diffusion on the full voxel space, we would need much more GPU memory than available, especially if we wanted to employ the attention mechanism. Additionally, compressing the data removes noisy small scale features from the data, which benefits us since a resolution of $0.039\Mpch$ is much smaller than the Jeans scale ($\approx1\Mpch$), and sub-Jeans scales carry negligible correlated physical information that would be smoothed away in the analysis regardless, a practice also implemented in other related works(\citet{Maitra_2026}). A third reason is that we found out that training on a compressed data point better preserves the long range correlations, especially in the case of $\vpec$ in this case. Last, since training the pipeline in the native resolution on the diffusion model takes days, compressing the data for the diffusion model drastically reduces training time. In our case, the training time went from about 50 hours to about 3 hours total.
\vspace{\baselineskip}

Hence, for this work, we decided to do a moderate compression by a factor of $20$, a sweet spot between very high compression, which could result in losing too much small scale information, and very light compression, which would result in no potential benefit of this added machinery. 
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth]{chapters//ch3_dps/VAE.pdf}
    \caption{The convolution structure of the VAE described in sec.~\ref{sec:VAE}. There are 3 downsampling blocks with the latent vector $\latent$ at the bottleneck. The \emph{spatial} dimensions are mentioned at each level.}
    \label{fig:VAE_compress}
\end{figure}
The architecture of the autoencoder is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:VAE_compress}. An important aspect of the architecture is that the downsampling performed in each encoder block is anisotropic, and is learned via strided convolutions. Across the three downsampling blocks, the transverse dimensions are reduced by a factor of 2 in each direction, while the line-of-sight dimension is reduced by a factor of 8. This choice preserves more of the spatial correlation structure in the transverse directions, which are initially sampled at a lower resolution compared to the line-of-sight direction.

The bottleneck of the VAE provides the compressed latent representation of the input data $\bm{x}_0$. Rather than encoding each input into a deterministic latent vector, the encoder parameterizes a Gaussian distribution in the latent space by predicting the mean $\bm{\mu}$ and standard deviation $\bm{\sigma}$ for each latent component. Consequently, the final encoder output contains twice the number of channels required for the latent representation, corresponding to the parameters of this distribution. During training, the latent representation $\bm{z}$ is sampled using the reparameterization trick:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:param_trick}
    \bm{z} = \bm{\mu} + \bm{\sigma}\bm{\epsilon},
    \qquad \bm{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}).
\end{equation}


\subsection{Training}
\label{sec:dps_training}
The training happens in 2 stages. In the first stage, a VAE(Sec.~\ref{sec:VAE}) is trained on the native resolution $5\times1024\times32\times32$ dimensional $\bm{x}_0$ data. After training, the VAE is frozen and the encoder is then used to encode all the training $\bm{x}_0$ into $8\times128\times16\times16$ dimensional latent $\latent$. The way the encoding happens is that we take the predicted $\bm{\mu}$ for each input $\bm{x}_0$ and set the corresponding $\bm{\sigma}$ to zero. This is done to ensure stable diffusion model training and reducing the variance coming from the VAE. From here on, this truncated $\bm{\mu}$ only latent space will be called $\latent$.
The diffusion prior is trained on this $\latent$ with the same variational diffusion model (VDM) objective used throughout this thesis (Section~\ref{sec:vdm} of Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi}): a learned, continuous-time noise schedule $\gamma(t)$ and the ELBO loss (Sec.~\ref{sec:vdm_elbo}). 
\subsubsection{Training the VAE}
\label{sec:VAE_training}

The VAE is trained in two stages for numerical stability. In Stage 1 (steps 0–2000), we minimize the L1 mean absolute error reconstruction loss and KL divergence (See Eq.~\ref{eq:kl_div}) terms:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{stage 1}} = \|\bm{x}_0 - \hat{\bm{x}}_0\|_1 + \beta\, \text{KL}\!\big(q_\phi(\latent|\bm{x}_0) \,\|\, p(\latent)\big),
\label{eq:vae_loss_stage1}
\end{equation}
where $\beta = 10^{-4}$ weights the KL term, $\hat{\bm{x}}_0$ is the reconstructed native field and $p(\latent)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{I})$.

In Stage 2 (steps 2,000–4,000), we introduce an additional spectral loss term with linear warmup to preserve fourier modes explicitly:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{stage 2}} = \|\bm{x}_0 - \hat{\bm{x}}_0\|_1 + \beta\, \text{KL}\!\big(q_\phi(\latent|\bm{x}_0) \,\|\, p(\latent)\big) + \lambda_{\text{spec}}(t)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}},
\label{eq:vae_loss_stage2}
\end{equation}


The spectral loss that penalizes mismatch in the 1D power spectrum along the line of sight:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}} = \sum_{c=1}^{5} w_c \left\langle \left[\log P_{\text{1D,rec}}^{(c)}(k) - \log P_{\text{1D,GT}}^{(c)}(k)\right]^2 \right\rangle,
\label{eq:spectral_loss}
\end{equation}
where the warmup schedule ramps the spectral weight from 0 to $\lambda_{\text{spec}} = 0.1$ over 500 steps. This warmup ramping helped stabilize training.

$P_{\text{1D,rec}}^{(c)}(k)$ and $P_{\text{1D,GT}}^{(c)}(k)$ are the 1D power spectra along the LoS axis for reconstructed and ground-truth fields respectively(computed via FFT and averaged over the transverse plane), the weights are $\bm{w} = [1.0, 1.0, 3.0, 1.0, 1.5]$ for channels $[\dmLOS, \gasLOS, \HILOS, T, \vpec]$. The heavier weight on $\HILOS$ ($w_2 = 3.0$) ensures faithful reconstruction of the ionization structure which directly couples to the observed Ly$\alpha$ absorption.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

For training, we use 8 3D simulations from the Sherwood simulations of side $40(\Mpch)^3$, gridded at $(1024,128,128)$ for each field, run with the same astrophysics and cosmology, but with different initial condition seed. Each of the 5 channels, corresponding to $[\dmLOS, \gasLOS, \HILOS, T, \vpec]$, are sub chunked into training samples $\bm{x}_0$ arrays of shape $(5,1024,32,32)$. To maximize the number of training data points, we create these chunks with overlapping volumes to get 625 chunks per simulation. This gives us a total of 5,000 sims for training and validating. We do a 70\%/30\% training/validation split.

\subsubsection{Training the latent VDM}
\label{sec:latent_vdm}
Now to train the VDM, we first freeze the encoder of the autoencoder and produce the 5,000 $\latent$ data points. At this point it must be mentioned that we are in fact reusing the same data used for training and validating the VAE, for this setup, since we are limited by the training sample size. Our VDM architecture closely follows Fig.~\ref{fig:DenoiseUnet} and the exact same machinery as described in Sec~\ref{sec:vdm} and~\ref{sec:vdm_elbo}. The only crucial changes are that instead of isotropically pooling the spatial dimensions at every downsampling block, we follow the same idea used in the VAE and perform anisotropic downsampling. There are 3 downsampling blocks with the following compression factors: $(2,2,2),(2,2,2),(2,1,1)$. So the first 2 blocks are isotropically downsampling by a factor of 2 for each axis, but the third block only downsamples the line of sight axis. Besides this, there are 8 input channels to the diffusion model. The training happens for a total of 450 epochs, with a 70\%/30\% training/validation split. The model weights with the lowest validation loss are stored.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

To get back the generated $\bm{x}_0$, we use the frozen decoder of the VAE on the diffusion model outputs.


\section{Theory of the Lyman-$\alpha$ Optical Depth}
\label{sec:lya-theory}


\subsection{Physical picture}
\label{sec:lya-physics}

A quasar or star-forming galaxy at redshift $z_s$ illuminates the intervening intergalactic medium along the line of sight. Each parcel of neutral hydrogen between source and observer resonantly scatters photons out of the line of sight near the rest-frame Ly$\alpha$ wavelength $\lambda_\alpha = 1215.6701\,\text{\AA}$. Gas at comoving position $x$ imprints absorption at a velocity (equivalently, wavelength) offset
\begin{equation}
u(x) = v_{\rm H}(x) + \vpec(x),
\label{eq:redshift-space}
\end{equation}
the sum of the local Hubble velocity $v_{\rm H}(x)=H(z)\,x_{\rm phys}$ and the gas's peculiar velocity along the line of sight, $\vpec(x)$. This redshift-space mapping is what allows $\vpec$ to distort the apparent position of absorption features relative to the real-space density peaks that sourced them.

Two independent physical effects broaden the absorption profile of a parcel of gas away from a single frequency:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Thermal Doppler broadening.} Hydrogen atoms at temperature $T$ have a Maxwell Boltzmann distribution of line-of-sight velocities, so photons resonant with atoms of different thermal velocity are absorbed at correspondingly shifted frequencies. This produces a Gaussian absorption profile of characteristic width (the Doppler parameter)
\begin{equation}
b = \sqrt{\frac{2k_B T}{m_H}}.
\label{eq:doppler-b}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Natural (radiative) damping.} The finite lifetime of the excited $2p$ state, set by the Einstein spontaneous-decay rate $\Gamma_{\rm Ly\alpha}=6.265\times10^8\,{\rm s^{-1}}$, gives the transition an intrinsic Lorentzian linewidth via Heisenberg's energy time uncertainty relation. This produces extended power law wings that fall off as $\Delta v^{-2}$ rather than exponentially, controlled by the dimensionless damping parameter
\begin{equation}
a = \frac{\Gamma_{\rm Ly\alpha}\,\lambda_\alpha}{4\pi b}.
\label{eq:voigt-a}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

The full line profile is the convolution of the Gaussian Doppler core with the Lorentzian damping wings , the \emph{Voigt profile}. Because $a\sim10^{-3}$ for Ly$\alpha$ at IGM temperatures, the profile is very nearly Gaussian near line center, with the damping term contributing only a small correction except deep in the wings.

A key consequence of this convolution is that the optical depth observed at a given pixel $i$ is \emph{not} a local function of the density at the corresponding position alone. Gas at every other pixel $j$ along the line of sight contributes to $\tau$ at pixel $i$, weighted by how far $j$'s absorption profile extends in velocity space to reach $i$. Sharp density peaks in real space are therefore smeared in velocity space, and peculiar velocities shift each absorption feature away from the real space location of its sourcing density peak due to Doppler effect. This is the origin of ``redshift-space distortions'' in the Ly$\alpha$ forest and is fully captured by treating $\tau$ as a discretized convolution rather than a pointwise map.

\subsection{The Voigt function and the Tepper-Garcia approximation}
\label{sec:voigt-hjerting}

The normalized Voigt line profile can be written in terms of the dimensionless velocity offset $x=(v-u)/b$ and damping parameter $a$ via the Voigt function $H(a,x)$, defined so that $H(0,x)=e^{-x^2}$ recovers the pure-Doppler Gaussian core. Direct numerical evaluation of $H(a,x)$ is expensive inside a differentiable, GPU-vectorized pipeline, so we use the closed-form approximation of \citet{tepper_2006},
\begin{equation}
H(a,x) \approx e^{-x^2} - \frac{a}{\sqrt{\pi}\,x^2}\left[e^{-2x^2}\!\left(4x^4+7x^2+4+\frac{3}{2x^2}\right)-\frac{3}{2x^2}-1\right],
\label{eq:tepper-garcia}
\end{equation}
which reduces smoothly to the Gaussian limit $H(a,x)\to e^{-x^2}$ as $x\to0$ and is accurate to better than $10^{-4}$ over the range of $a$ and $x$ relevant to the IGM. 

\subsection{Discretized optical depth along a sightline}
\label{sec:tau-discretized}

Combining the physical picture of Section~\ref{sec:lya-physics} with the Tepper-Garcia profile, the Ly$\alpha$ optical depth at velocity $v_i$ is the sum, over every pixel $j$ along the sightline, of that pixel's contribution to the absorption at $v_i$:
\begin{equation}
\tau(v_i) = \sum_j \frac{c\,\sigma_{\rm Ly\alpha}\,\Delta x_{\rm phys}\,\bar n_{\rm H}(z)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\frac{\Delta_{{\rm gas},j}\,x_{{\rm HI},j}}{b_j}\;\; H\!\left(a_j,\,\frac{v_i-u_j}{b_j}\right),
\label{eq:tau-sum}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\rm Ly\alpha}=\sqrt{3\pi\sigma_T/8}\,\lambda_\alpha f_{\rm osc}$ is the frequency-integrated Ly$\alpha$ absorption cross-section written in terms of the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_T$ and oscillator strength $f_{\rm osc}=0.4164$, $\Delta x_{\rm phys}$ is the physical width of one sightline pixel at redshift $z$, and $\bar n_{\rm H}(z)$ is the mean physical hydrogen number density obtained from the cosmological parameters,
\begin{equation}
\bar n_{\rm H}(z) = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}\,\frac{\Omega_b X_h}{(1+z)^3\,m_H}, \qquad H_0 = 100\,h\;{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}.
\label{eq:mean-nH}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:tau-sum} is precisely the standard Gunn,Peterson-type optical depth expression, evaluated \emph{directly} from the hydrodynamical simulation's $\gasLOS$, $\HILOS$, and $T$ fields rather than from an FGPA-style  relating $\HILOS$ and $T$ to $\gasLOS$ alone through a fixed power-law equation of state. This is the same distinction drawn in Section~\ref{sec:dps_intro} between our forward model and the FGPA-based forward model used in TARDIS \citep{horowitz_2019}: by construction, none of the small-scale baryonic physics (pressure smoothing, shock heating, patchy reionization) needs to be re-derived analytically, since it is already encoded in the simulation fields that Eq.~\ref{eq:tau-sum} consumes directly.

Because the simulation box is periodic, the velocity separation $|v_i-u_j|$ appearing inside $H$ is wrapped onto the interval $[0,v_{\rm max}/2]$, where
\begin{equation}
v_{\rm max} = H(z)\,L_{\rm box,phys} = H(z)\,\frac{L_{\rm box}\,(1+z)^{-1}}{h}
\label{eq:vmax}
\end{equation}
is the total Hubble velocity spanned by one period of the box; separations larger than $v_{\rm max}/2$ are replaced by $v_{\rm max}$ minus themselves before evaluating Eq.~\ref{eq:tepper-garcia}.

Equation~\ref{eq:tau-sum} is evaluated as a dense $n_{\rm bins}\times n_{\rm bins}$ pairwise sum per sightline, the discretized analogue of the convolution integral $\tau(v) = \int dx\,n_{\rm HI}(x)\,\sigma_{\rm Ly\alpha}\,\phi(v-u(x))$, implemented as a fully vectorized, GPU-based and automatically differentiable operation, a requirement for its later use as the likelihood term in diffusion posterior sampling (Section~\ref{sec:dps_theory}).



\section{Diffusion Posterior Sampling}
\label{sec:dps_theory}

We now turn to the chapter's core methodological contribution: how to combine the unconditional prior of Section~\ref{sec:dps_architecture} with the differentiable forward model of Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory} to sample from the posterior over the joint field, $p(\bm{x}_0\mid\bm{y})$, given an observed (noisy) Ly$\alpha$ flux spectrum $\bm{y}$ along one or more sightlines , without ever training a network that has seen $\bm y$ or any similar observation.

\subsection{Specializing the general formulation to Lyman-alpha tomography}
\label{sec:dps_guided}

The general diffusion posterior sampling recipe was set out in Section~\ref{sec:dps_formulation} of the Introduction. In brief: at each reverse step the score of the noised posterior decomposes, by Bayes' theorem, into the pretrained prior score plus a likelihood-guidance term (Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_score_decomposition}), and the guidance term is made tractable by replacing the intractable $p(\bm{x}_0\mid\bm{z}_t)$ with a point mass at the Tweedie estimate of the clean field (Eqs.~\ref{eq:dps_delta_approx}--\ref{eq:dps_likelihood_approx}), so that $p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{z}_t)\approx p(\bm{y}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t))$. Here the prior is the unconditional five-field variational diffusion model of Section~\ref{sec:dps_architecture}, and the guidance term is the only place the observed Ly$\alpha$ flux $\bm{y}$, the sightline geometry, and the forward model of Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory} enter the sampling procedure at all. The remainder of this section builds and explains the two problem specific pieces the general recipe leaves open. That is the explicit likelihood $p(\bm{y}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_0)$ for this forward model, and the resulting guidance gradient and sampling algorithm.

\subsection{The Lyman-$\alpha$ likelihood}
\label{sec:dps_tweedie}

In this chapter's variational-diffusion notation, the clean-field estimate entering the likelihood approximation is the identity already introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi} (Eq.~\ref{eq:tweedie}), and generally as Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_xhat},
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t) \;=\; \frac{\bm{z}_t - \sigma_t\,\hat{\bm\epsilon}_{\bm\theta}(\bm{z}_t,t)}{\alpha_t}\,.
\label{eq:dps_tweedie}
\end{equation}
This is exactly the Tweedie estimate written generally in the Introduction as Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_xhat}, only in the variational-diffusion notation of Chapter~\ref{ch:lodi}. The VDM writes the forward process as $\bm{z}_t = \alpha_t\bm{x}_0 + \sigma_t\bm{\epsilon}$, so its signal and noise coefficients $(\alpha_t,\sigma_t)$ are the continuous time, variance preserving counterparts of the DDPM factors $(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t},\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t})$ used there, $\alpha_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$. The clean field estimate is therefore the same object in both.

Modeling the observational noise on the flux as Gaussian with variance $\sigma_y^2$, set by the survey's spectral signal to noise ratio, turns the general approximation $p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{z}_t)\approx p(\bm{y}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t))$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_likelihood_approx}) into an explicit, differentiable form,
\begin{equation}
p\!\big(\bm{y}\mid\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t)\big) \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_y^2}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\mathcal{A}\!\big(\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t)\big)\right\rVert^2\right),
\label{eq:dps_gaussian_likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}(\cdot)$ denotes the full forward operator of Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory}: extracting the four gas-phase channels $(\gasLOS,\HILOS,T,\vpec)$ from the joint field estimate (recall that $\dmLOS$ never enters $\mathcal{A}$ directly; Section~\ref{sec:lya-physics}), evaluating the discretised optical depth of Eq.~\ref{eq:tau-sum}, and converting to transmitted flux via $F=e^{-\tau}$.

\subsection{The guidance gradient and sampling algorithm}
\label{sec:dps_algorithm}

Combining Eqs.~\ref{eq:dps_likelihood_approx}, \ref{eq:dps_gaussian_likelihood}, the guidance term of Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_score_decomposition} becomes
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\bm{z}_t}\log p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{z}_t) \;\approx\; -\zeta_t\,\nabla_{\bm{z}_t}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\mathcal{A}\big(\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t)\big)\right\rVert^2,
\label{eq:dps_guidance}
\end{equation}
where the gradient is taken with respect to $\bm{z}_t$ by backpropagating through both the Tweedie estimate $\hat{\bm x}_0(\bm z_t)$ (i.e.\ through the denoising network itself) and the differentiable forward model $\mathcal{A}$ (Section~\ref{sec:tau-discretized} was built explicitly to make this possible). We absorb $1/\sigma_y^2$ and the normalization of the Tweedie estimate into a single scalar \emph{guidance strength} $\zeta_t$, tuned and scheduled empirically rather than fixed analytically, the Gaussian likelihood derivation above is exact only in the $t\to0$ limit, and $\zeta_t$ in practice compensates for the approximation error at earlier, noisier steps. For our work, $\zeta_t$ follows a schedule given by:

\begin{equation}
\zeta_t = \zeta\,\frac{1-\cos\left(\pi(1-t)\right)}{2}.
\label{eq:zeta_schedule}
\end{equation}
This ensures that when $t\rightarrow1$, $\zeta_t $ is close to zero, since the estimate of $\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t)$ is very noisy and we do not want it to push the guidance in the wrong direction, hence the weak guidance strength. On the other hand, for $t\rightarrow0$, $\zeta_t$ reaches the maximum value of $\zeta$, which for this work is $\zeta=10$. It was chosen by empirical testing. Too high of a guidance strength pushes the diffusion model out of its learned prior region and causes the outputs to become unphysical (deviate from the prior correlation structure). A weak guidance, on the other hand, does not get guided enough by the observation $\bm{y}$, and produces outputs that weakly correlate with it.
Each reverse step then combines the ordinary unconditional update of Eq.~\ref{eq:reverse_step} with this additional gradient, pulling the reverse process, at every step, towards states consistent with both the learned prior and the observed flux:
\begin{equation}
\bm{z}_{t-1} = \big[\text{unconditional reverse step, Eq.~\ref{eq:reverse_step}}\big] \;-\; \zeta_t\,\nabla_{\bm{z}_t}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\mathcal{A}\big(\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t)\big)\right\rVert^2.
\label{eq:dps_reverse_step}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption{Diffusion posterior sampling for joint five-field reconstruction}
\label{alg:dps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\bm{z}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$
\For{$t = T,\dots,1$}
    \State $\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t) \gets \big(\bm{z}_t - \sigma_t\,\hat{\bm\epsilon}_{\bm\theta}(\bm{z}_t,t)\big)/\alpha_t$ \Comment{Tweedie estimate, Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_tweedie}}
    \State $\bm{z}_{t-1}' \gets$ unconditional reverse step from $\bm{z}_t$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:reverse_step}}
    \State $g \gets \nabla_{\bm{z}_t}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\mathcal{A}\big(\hat{\bm{x}}_0(\bm{z}_t)\big)\right\rVert^2$ \Comment{backprop through network \emph{and} forward model}
    \State $\bm{z}_{t-1} \gets \bm{z}_{t-1}' - \zeta_t\, g$ \Comment{Eq.~\ref{eq:dps_reverse_step}}
\EndFor
\State \textbf{return} $\bm{z}_0$ \Comment{one posterior sample of the joint five-field state}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\section{Results}
\label{sec:dps_results}
To showcase the preliminary results, we present the reconstruction of the baryonic and dark matter fields, having observed only a single realization of the Lyman-$\alpha$ flux. We test two different scenarios:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We vary the observed sightline sampling density.
    \item We vary the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the flux observations.
\end{itemize}
All these tests are done on an unseen test simulation. The test simulation has the same cosmology and astrophysics as the test simulations but was run with a different initial condition seed.
Note that, as mentioned, we do not need to retrain our model to perform these various tests, since the observed flux never enters the training pipeline. Since these are preliminary results, we only show the outputs for a subvolume of the full simulation of shape $40\Mpch\times10\Mpch\times10\Mpch$. For each combination, we generate 10 posterior samples of shape $(5,1024,32,32)$, matching our training data resolution and spanning a single chunk of the full simulation volume.

Our analysis proceeds in two stages. First, we perform 1D Gaussian smoothing along the line of sight only, isolating the quality of individual sightline reconstructions without transverse mixing. This reveals how well the DPS recovers velocity structure and density perturbations on individual sightlines. Second, we apply full 3D smoothing to mix information across transverse neighbours, examining whether the large-scale 3D structure and correlations are faithfully reconstructed.

\textbf{Noise model:} We add a simple, flux-dependent noise to the synthetic observations. Rather than assuming constant noise, we adopt a spatially-varying noise model where the noise standard deviation at each pixel is inversely proportional to the local flux,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:SNR}
\sigma_{\text{noise}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{F(\mathbf{r})}{\text{SNR}},
\end{equation}
with a minimum floor of $\sigma_{\min} = 10^{-4}$ to avoid numerical issues in deep absorption features. This ensures that absorption troughs and high-transmission regions have comparable effective SNR, rather than a constant noise everywhere.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:dps_metrics}
Before presenting the results, we define the summary statistics used throughout this section.

\textbf{1D power spectrum.} For a field $f$ defined on the LoS grid, we compute the 1D power spectrum along the line of sight, averaged over all transverse sightlines within the sub-volume,
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm 1D}(k) = \frac{L_{\rm LoS}}{N_{\rm LoS}^2}\left\langle \left|\widetilde{\delta f}(k)\right|^2 \right\rangle_{\perp},
\label{eq:pk1d}
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{\delta f}(k)$ is the discrete Fourier transform of the overdensity field along the LoS axis, $L_{\rm LoS} = 40\Mpch$ is the box length along the line of sight, $N_{\rm LoS}=1024$ is the number of LoS pixels, and $\langle\cdot\rangle_\perp$ denotes averaging over the transverse $(32\times32)$ sightlines. This statistic quantifies how power is distributed across scales along the line of sight, and lets us check whether the reconstruction preserves the correct clustering amplitude at each $k$. A diffusion trained prior should be able to learn the multiscale power of the fields in principle.

\textbf{Pearson correlation coefficient.} To quantify how linearly related the reconstructed and ground-truth fields are, we use the Pearson correlation coefficient,
\begin{equation}
r_{\rm P} = \frac{\sum_i (g_i - \bar{g})(l_i - \bar{l})}{\sqrt{\sum_i (g_i - \bar{g})^2}\sqrt{\sum_i (l_i - \bar{l})^2}},
\label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}
where $g_i$ and $l_i$ are the ground-truth and latent-DPS field values at voxel $i$, and $\bar{g}$, $\bar{l}$ are their respective means. $r_{\rm P}$ ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with $r_{\rm P}=1$ indicating a perfect linear relationship. Because $r_{\rm P}$ is sensitive to the absolute scale and outliers of the field, we compute it after applying the appropriate transform for each channel (linear for $\delta_{\rm DM}$ and $v_{\rm pec}$, $\log_{10}$ for $\Delta_{\rm gas}$, $x_{\rm HI}$, and $T$, so that the statistic is not dominated by the multiplicative dynamic range of the underlying physical quantities.

\textbf{Spearman correlation coefficient.} As a complementary, rank-based statistic, we also compute the Spearman correlation coefficient,
\begin{equation}
r_{\rm S} = 1 - \frac{6\sum_i d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)},
\label{eq:spearman}
\end{equation}
where $d_i$ is the difference between the ranks of $g_i$ and $l_i$ within their respective distributions, and $n$ is the number of voxels compared. Unlike $r_{\rm P}$, $r_{\rm S}$ only requires that the reconstruction preserve the correct \emph{ordering} of field values, and is therefore more robust to monotonic but non-linear biases. For example, if 2 fields fluctuate the same way but their amplitudes are different, Pearson correlation would be smaller than the corresponding Spearman correlation coefficient. Comparing $r_{\rm P}$ and $r_{\rm S}$ side by side lets us disentangle whether a drop in correlation is due to genuine structural mismatch or simply an amplitude/normalization offset.

Both coefficients are computed per posterior sample and then aggregated across the 10 samples.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:dps_discussion}

\subsection{What the results show}
\label{sec:dps_disc_summary}

The results of Section~\ref{sec:dps_results} demonstrate that a single, unconditionally trained diffusion prior over the joint five-field state can be turned, at inference time alone, into a working posterior sampler for Lyman-$\alpha$ tomography, across a range of sightline densities and noise levels, with no retraining between cases, even working with just one set of observations. The 1D power spectra of Figures~\ref{fig:pk1d_3cases_5} and~\ref{fig:pk1d_3cases_50} show that two-point statistics are recovered accurately down to the non-linear scales where the forward model itself becomes most sensitive to the small-scale gas physics, and the correlation coefficients of Figure~\ref{fig:corr_2mpc} confirm this holds voxel-by-voxel, not only in a statistically averaged sense. As expected, both figures also show where the method is pushed hardest: fidelity degrades smoothly as the observed sightline fraction drops, most visibly in the high-density $\dmLOS$ peaks of Figure~\ref{fig:los_3cases}, where the guidance term has the least direct observational support to draw on and the reverse process falls back increasingly on the learned prior.

\subsection{The dark matter recovery is the result that matters most}
\label{sec:dps_disc_dm}

It is worth stating again that of the five reconstructed fields, $\dmLOS$ is the only one that never enters the forward model $\mathcal{A}$ of Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory} at all. Its recovery is therefore mediated entirely by whatever correlations the diffusion prior learned, during unconditional training on the Sherwood-Relics simulations, between the dark matter field and the four gas-phase fields the likelihood actually constrains. That the correlation coefficients for $\dmLOS$ in Figure~\ref{fig:corr_2mpc} track those of the directly-constrained fields as closely as they do is not a statement about the forward model at all, it is a direct, quantitative statement about how much of the dark matter, gas coupling learned by the network reflects genuine structure-formation physics (gravitational infall of gas onto the same potential wells that host the dark matter overdensity) rather than a artifact of training. This is arguably the chapter's most novel result, since it is not achievable by any forward model only or FGPA-based method such as TARDIS~\citep{horowitz_2019,horowitz_2021}, which has no mechanism for inferring a field that is absent from its own likelihood. 

\subsection{Limitations of the current results}
\label{sec:dps_disc_limits}

The results presented here are, as stated at the top of the chapter, preliminary in three specific, related ways that motivate the future work below. First, they cover a single $40\times10\times10\,(\Mpch)^3$ sub-volume, one $1024\times32\times32$ training-resolution chunk, which is $1/16$ of the total volume of the simulation, rather than the full $40\times40\times40\,(\Mpch)^3$ simulation box. Second, the sightline density sweep of Section~\ref{sec:dps_results} tests robustness to \emph{missing} observations by masking sightlines out of the same dense, fixed $32\times32$ training grid, not robustness to a genuinely different \emph{native} sightline geometry of the kind a sparser real survey would deliver. Third, both the VAE (Section~\ref{sec:VAE_training}) and the latent diffusion prior (Section~\ref{sec:latent_vdm}) are trained on the same 5,000 chunks drawn from only 8 independent simulations, differing only in initial-condition seed rather than in astrophysics or cosmology. This however, is not a major issue since for testing we do use an unseen simulation, so any biases generated during training would be visible at test time. In particular, none of these limitations is expected to undermine the dark matter recovery result of Section~\ref{sec:dps_disc_dm}: that result rests on correlations the prior learned between dark matter and the gas-phase fields, which more independent training simulations should reinforce rather than weaken, so the qualitative finding are robust even though the quoted fidelity numbers are not yet final.


\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:dps_disc_future}

\paragraph{Generating the full simulation box.} The most immediate extension is to move from the single sub-volume shown here to the full $40\times40\times40\,(\Mpch)^3$ box: 16 tiles of the current sub-volume's transverse footprint (a factor of 4 in each transverse direction) would be needed to cover it. This is exactly the problem that \FOLD's field-overlap latent diffusion (Chapter~\ref{ch:cosmofold}) were built to solve for this thesis's other diffusion models, and the same overlap-and-blend strategy is a natural candidate here: generate overlapping transverse tiles independently under DPS guidance, then blend them in the overlap region to enforce large scale coherence across tile boundaries, rather than treating each tile as an independent posterior draw. 

\paragraph{Training at native lower sightline densities.} The sparsity tests in Section~\ref{sec:dps_results} mask sightlines out of a fixed, dense $32\times32$ grid at inference time, they test what happens when observational \emph{coverage} of an otherwise well-resolved grid is incomplete, not what happens when the underlying survey's native sightline separation is itself coarser than $0.31\,\hMpci$. Real tomographic surveys such as DESI Ly$\alpha$ QSO or WEAVE-QSO (Section~\ref{sec:dps_intro}) have substantially sparser mean sightline separations than Subaru-PFS/CLAMATO-class surveys, and it is not guaranteed that a prior and forward model built around the current training grid's resolution generalize to that regime by masking alone: the VAE's anisotropic downsampling (Section~\ref{sec:VAE}) and the diffusion prior's own learned correlations were both shaped by what the training chunks actually look like at $32\times32$ transverse resolution. Training a variant of the prior directly on chunks built from a coarser native transverse grid, and comparing its reconstruction fidelity against the current model evaluated on the same coarse case via masking alone, would directly test whether retraining at the target survey's actual sightline density is necessary, or whether the current no-retraining claim (Section~\ref{sec:dps_intro}) genuinely holds across geometries and not only across coverage fractions of one fixed geometry.

\paragraph{Training with more data.} The current prior is trained on 5,000 overlapping chunks drawn from only 8 independent simulations sharing one astrophysical and cosmological model. The overlapping-window chunking scheme (Section~\ref{sec:VAE_training}) means many of these 5,000 samples are not independent draws, so the effective diversity seen by the network during training is smaller than the raw count suggests. An extension follows directly: more independent random-seed realizations at fixed cosmology and astrophysics, to test whether current fidelity is limited by training-set diversity rather than model capacity

\paragraph{Optimizing the latent space.} The $20\times$ compression factor used throughout this chapter (Section~\ref{sec:VAE}) was chosen qualitatively, as a "sweet spot" between over-compressing away small-scale information and under-compressing enough to make no computational difference, rather than from a systematic ablation. An important next step is to quantify the fidelity, compute trade-off directly. Train VAEs at a range of compression factors and anisotropic downsampling schedules, and evaluate each against the same reconstruction metrics already used in Section~\ref{sec:dps_metrics} (power spectrum residuals, Pearson/Spearman correlation), rather than relying on the qualitative reasoning of Section~\ref{sec:VAE} alone. This would also clarify whether a single, fixed compression factor is even the right choice, or whether a non-uniform/adaptive latent representation, allocating more of the latent budget to the fields or scales that the guidance term relies on most directly ($\gasLOS$, $\HILOS$ given their role in the optical-depth forward model of Section~\ref{sec:lya-theory}), could improve reconstruction fidelity at the same overall compression.
